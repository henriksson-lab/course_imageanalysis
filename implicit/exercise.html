Exercise: curves as level sets

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • read inside and outside from the sign of \phi and build shapes with min and max,
  • compute the normal and the curvature of a level line from \phi, by hand on a small grid,
  • say what a signed distance function is, and why many functions \phi describe the same curve,
  • explain why the topology of a contour can change while \phi changes only a little,
  • (math) derive the curvature of a circle from \nabla\cdot(\nabla\phi/|\nabla\phi|), and see where min and max stop being distances.

Do the pen-and-paper part first, then the demo. Questions marked (math) are for those who want the derivations; the concept questions do not depend on them.

Background

Idea What it means
Implicit curve the set where \phi(x, y) = 0; \phi < 0 inside, \phi > 0 outside
Union, intersection, complement \min(\phi_1, \phi_2), \max(\phi_1, \phi_2), -\phi
Outward normal \mathbf n = \nabla\phi / \lvert\nabla\phi\rvert
Curvature \kappa = \nabla\cdot\mathbf n; a circle of radius r has \kappa = 1/r
Signed distance function \lvert\phi\rvert = distance to the curve; then \lvert\nabla\phi\rvert = 1, \mathbf n = \nabla\phi, \kappa = \nabla^2\phi
Central differences \phi_x \approx \tfrac12(\phi_{i+1} - \phi_{i-1}), \phi_{xx} \approx \phi_{i+1} - 2\phi_i + \phi_{i-1} (pixel spacing 1)
On the grid the contour lies between pixels where the sign changes (marching squares, from the marching-cubes day)
The math: curvature from φ

\kappa = \nabla\cdot\frac{\nabla\phi}{|\nabla\phi|} = \frac{\phi_{xx}\phi_y^2 - 2\phi_x\phi_y\phi_{xy} + \phi_{yy}\phi_x^2}{(\phi_x^2 + \phi_y^2)^{3/2}}, with the mixed derivative by central differences \phi_{xy} \approx \tfrac14(\phi_{i+1,j+1} - \phi_{i+1,j-1} - \phi_{i-1,j+1} + \phi_{i-1,j-1}). Where \phi_y = 0 this reduces to \kappa = \phi_{yy}/|\phi_x|.

By hand

The grid below samples \phi = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} - 1.5 at x, y \in \{-2, -1, 0, 1, 2\} (rounded to 3 decimals):

y \backslash x −2 −1 0 1 2
2 1.328 0.736 0.500 0.736 1.328
1 0.736 −0.086 −0.500 −0.086 0.736
0 0.500 −0.500 −1.500 −0.500 0.500
−1 0.736 −0.086 −0.500 −0.086 0.736
−2 1.328 0.736 0.500 0.736 1.328

B1. (a) Mark every pixel inside the curve. How many are there? (b) Along the row y = 0, the sign changes between x = 1 and x = 2. Interpolate linearly to find where the curve crosses. Compare with the true circle. (c) The same along the row y = 1, between x = 1 and x = 2. True crossing: x = \sqrt{1.5^2 - 1}.

B2. At the pixel (1, 0): (a) compute \phi_x and \phi_y by central differences, |\nabla\phi|, and the normal; (b) compute \phi_{yy} and the curvature \kappa = \phi_{yy}/|\phi_x| (here \phi_y = 0). Compare with the exact value 1/\rho, where \rho is the distance of the pixel from the centre.

B3. At the pixel (1, 1): compute \phi_x, \phi_y and the normal by central differences. Compare the normal with the exact one, (x, y)/\rho. Is the estimate of |\nabla\phi| also exact? Why might it not be?

B4. Set operations. (a) Write a \phi for a ring (annulus) between the circles of radius 1 and 2 around the origin, using \max. (b) Evaluate it at the origin, at \rho = 1.5 and at \rho = 3. Inside or outside? (c) Is your \phi a signed distance function? Check one point in each of the three regions.

B5. Lines. (a) Show that \phi = (3x + 4y - 10)/5 is a signed distance function (what is |\nabla\phi|?). Which side of the line is “inside”? (b) How far is the origin from the line? Read it from \phi. (c) The same line is also the zero set of 3x + 4y - 10. What changes, and what does not?

B6. Predict. An ellipse with semi-axes a = 2.2 (along x) and b = 1.2. At which points is the curvature largest, and at which smallest? The exact values are a/b^2 and b/a^2: compute them, and the radius of the circle that fits the curve best at each point.

Solutions

B1. (a) The 9 pixels with negative values: the centre, its 4 neighbours (-0.5) and the 4 diagonal neighbours (-0.086). (b) \phi(1, 0) = -0.5, \phi(2, 0) = 0.5: the crossing is halfway, at x = 1.5: exactly the radius (along this row \phi is linear in x). (c) \phi(1, 1) = -0.086, \phi(2, 1) = 0.736: t = 0.086/0.822 = 0.104, so x = 1.104. The true crossing is \sqrt{1.25} = 1.118: linear interpolation is off by 0.014 because \phi bends between the two pixels.

B2. (a) \phi_x = (\phi(2, 0) - \phi(0, 0))/2 = (0.5 + 1.5)/2 = 1, \phi_y = (\phi(1, 1) - \phi(1, -1))/2 = 0: |\nabla\phi| = 1, normal (1, 0): exact. (b) \phi_{yy} = \phi(1, 1) - 2\phi(1, 0) + \phi(1, -1) = -0.086 + 1 - 0.086 = 0.828, so \kappa \approx 0.83. The pixel is at \rho = 1: the level line through it is a circle of radius 1 with \kappa = 1. The coarse grid underestimates the curvature by 17%.

B3. \phi_x = (\phi(2, 1) - \phi(0, 1))/2 = (0.736 + 0.5)/2 = 0.618, and by symmetry \phi_y = 0.618. Normal (0.618, 0.618)/0.874 = (0.707, 0.707): exactly (1, 1)/\sqrt 2. But |\nabla\phi| = 0.874, not 1: the central difference averages the slope over two pixels, and the distance function bends in between (it has a kink at the centre, two pixels away).

B4. (a) Inside the ring means inside the big disk and outside the small one: \phi = \max(\rho - 2,\ 1 - \rho) with \rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}. (b) Origin: \max(-2, 1) = 1, outside (in the hole). \rho = 1.5: \max(-0.5, -0.5) = -0.5, inside, 0.5 from both circles. \rho = 3: \max(1, -2) = 1, outside. (c) Yes: in the hole 1 - \rho is the distance to the inner circle, outside \rho - 2 is the distance to the outer one, and in the ring the larger of the two (the one closer to zero) is minus the distance to the nearer circle.

B5. (a) \nabla\phi = (3, 4)/5, length 1. Inside is where 3x + 4y < 10: the side containing the origin. (b) \phi(0, 0) = -2: the origin is 2 from the line, on the inside. (c) The curve, the inside and the normal direction are the same; |\nabla\phi| = 5, so the values are 5 times the distances.

B6. Largest at the ends of the long axis, (\pm 2.2, 0), where the curve turns sharply: \kappa = 2.2/1.2^2 = 1.53, radius 1/\kappa = 0.65. Smallest at (0, \pm 1.2), where it is flattest: \kappa = 1.2/2.2^2 = 0.248, radius 4.03.

Math questions

B7. (math) For \phi = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} - r, compute \nabla\phi and \nabla\cdot(\nabla\phi/|\nabla\phi|), and show that every level line has curvature 1/\rho.

B8. (math) For \phi = x^2 + y^2 - r^2: compute |\nabla\phi| and \nabla^2\phi. Show that the formula \kappa = \nabla\cdot(\nabla\phi/|\nabla\phi|) still gives 1/\rho, but \nabla^2\phi does not.

B9. (math) Two disks of radius 1 centred at (\pm 0.5, 0) overlap. (a) Evaluate \min(\phi_1, \phi_2) (signed distances of the two disks) at the origin. (b) What is the true distance from the origin to the boundary of the union? (c) Explain why \min of two signed distances is exact outside the union but not always inside.

Solutions (math)

B7. \nabla\phi = (x, y)/\rho with \rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}; |\nabla\phi| = 1. \partial_x(x/\rho) = 1/\rho - x^2/\rho^3 = y^2/\rho^3 and \partial_y(y/\rho) = x^2/\rho^3, so \kappa = (x^2 + y^2)/\rho^3 = 1/\rho. The level line through a point at distance \rho is the circle of radius \rho: its curvature is 1/\rho, on the curve itself 1/r.

B8. \nabla\phi = 2(x, y), |\nabla\phi| = 2\rho, \nabla^2\phi = 2 + 2 = 4. The unit normal is 2(x, y)/(2\rho) = (x, y)/\rho, the same as in B7, so its divergence is again 1/\rho. The Laplacian, 4, is not a curvature at all: \kappa = \nabla^2\phi needs |\nabla\phi| = 1.

B9. (a) \phi_1(0, 0) = \phi_2(0, 0) = 0.5 - 1 = -0.5, so \min = -0.5. (b) The boundary of the union consists of the parts of each circle outside the other disk; the nearest such points to the origin are the two intersection points (0, \pm\sqrt{1 - 0.25}) = (0, \pm 0.866). True signed distance: -0.866. (c) Outside the union, the nearest boundary point of the union is the nearest point of the nearer circle, and that point lies on the union’s boundary: \min is exact. Inside, the nearest point of a circle may lie inside the other disk (from the origin, the nearest point of circle 1 is (-0.5, 0), which lies inside disk 2), so it is not on the union’s boundary; \min then underestimates the depth. The zero set is still right, which is all a level set needs; reinitialisation (unit 2) repairs the values.

At (0, 0) (a saddle) and at (1, 1) (a maximum) the gradient is zero: there is no direction “across” the level line, so no normal and no curvature. At such points the topology of the contours changes.

Where this leads

So far \phi was fixed and we moved the cutting plane. The next unit keeps the cut at \phi = 0 and changes \phi instead, a little at every time step, so that the curve moves with a chosen speed: the level set equation. Merging and splitting will come for free, for the reason seen in E2.
