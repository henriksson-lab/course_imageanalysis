Exercise: filtered back-projection and sampling

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • filter a projection with a (truncated) ramp kernel by hand and explain the negative sidelobes,
  • explain why the filter is a ramp, why it amplifies noise, and what a tapered ramp trades,
  • use the angle rule N_\theta \approx \tfrac{\pi}{2}N and predict streaks,
  • predict the missing wedge of a limited tilt range and its effect on an object.

Do the pen-and-paper part first, then the two demos. Questions marked (math) are for those who want the derivations; the concept questions do not depend on them.

Background

Idea What it means
FBP filter every projection with a ramp, then back-project, times \pi/N_\theta
Ramp multiplies the projection’s 1D spectrum by |\omega|; as a kernel (Ram–Lak): h[0] = \tfrac14, h[k] = -1/(\pi k)^2 for odd k, 0 for even k \ne 0
Taper Shepp–Logan, cosine, Hann: multiply the ramp so it rolls off to 0 at the cutoff
Zero padding extend each projection with zeros to at least twice its length before filtering by FFT
Angle rule N detector bins need about \tfrac{\pi}{2}N angles over 180°
Missing wedge a tilt range of \pm\alpha leaves a wedge of 180° - 2\alpha in the spectrum empty

By hand

B1. The truncated ramp kernel (-0.1,\ 0.25,\ -0.1). (a) Filter the box projection (0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0) (zeros outside). Where is the result largest, where negative? (b) Filter the wider box (0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0). What happens inside a wide object? (c) Explain in words how the negative values just outside the object remove the glow of plain back-projection.

B2. The full Ram–Lak kernel. (a) Compute h[0], h[1], h[2] and h[3]. (b) The sum of the kernel over |k| \le K is \tfrac14 - 2\sum_{k \text{ odd} \le K}\frac{1}{\pi^2k^2}. Compute it for K = 1 and K = 3. What is the limit, and why must it be so for a ramp? (c) Why is the three-tap kernel of B1 not quite right for a wide, flat object?

B3. The angle rule. (a) How many angles over 180° does a detector with 512 bins need? With 256? (b) A scan of a 256-bin detector uses 90 angles. What artefact do you expect, and where in the image is it strongest?

B4. An electron microscope records a tilt series from -60° to +60° in steps of 2°; the beam is vertical at 0° tilt. (a) How many images are recorded? Which sector of the 2D spectrum stays empty, and how wide is it? (b) The same with a range of \pm 70°.

B5. Same tilt series as B4. Sketch what a thin horizontal line and a thin vertical line look like after reconstruction. Which one suffers, and why? What happens to a disc?

B6. Noise. (a) The noise variance of FBP grows like the cube of the cutoff frequency. By what factor does the noise standard deviation change if you halve the cutoff? (b) By what factor does it change if you double the number of photons per ray (shot noise: the variance of a line integral is about 1/counts)? (c) Why is it expensive in dose to halve the pixel size?

Solutions

B1. (a) (0,\ -0.1,\ 0.15,\ 0.05,\ 0.15,\ -0.1,\ 0). Largest at the two edges of the box (0.15), smaller inside (0.05), negative just outside (−0.1). (b) (0,\ -0.1,\ 0.15,\ 0.05,\ 0.05,\ 0.05,\ 0.15,\ -0.1,\ 0): the interior is flat at 0.05; only the edges are emphasised. (c) Back-projected, the negative values make a negative band along both sides of each smear. Around the object the smears from all the other angles leave a positive glow there; the negative bands cancel it. Inside, the positive centre parts add up to the object’s value.

B2. (a) h[0] = 0.25, h[1] = -1/\pi^2 = -0.101, h[2] = 0, h[3] = -1/(9\pi^2) = -0.011. (b) K = 1: 0.25 - 0.203 = 0.047; K = 3: 0.25 - 0.225 = 0.025. The limit is 0 (with \sum_{\text{odd}} 1/k^2 = \pi^2/8). The ramp is 0 at frequency 0, so a constant input must give 0: the kernel must sum to 0. (c) The three taps sum to 0.05, not 0: every flat part keeps 0.05 times its value, a low-frequency error that the far-reaching small negative taps would remove. For a wide object that leftover is a slow bump in the reconstruction.

B3. (a) \tfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot 512 = 804, about 800 angles; for 256 bins about 400. (b) Far too few (about a quarter): streaks radiating from high-contrast edges, strongest around dense or bright structures and towards the edge of the field of view, where neighbouring spokes are farthest apart.

B4. (a) (60 - (-60))/2 + 1 = 61 images. The spokes cover 120°; a 60° wedge around the beam direction (the vertical frequency axis) stays empty. (b) 71 images; the empty wedge is 40° wide.

B5. The horizontal line suffers: its spectrum lies along the vertical frequency axis, inside the missing wedge, so it is smeared out along the beam (vertically) and loses contrast, or nearly vanishes. The vertical line’s spectrum lies along the horizontal axis, which is measured: it stays sharp. A disc is stretched along the beam and its top and bottom edges blur: a lemon.

B6. (a) Variance \times 1/8, standard deviation \times 1/\sqrt8 = 0.35 (2.8 times smaller). (b) Variance halves, standard deviation \times 1/\sqrt2 = 0.71. (c) Half the pixel size needs twice the cutoff frequency (8 times the variance) and each detector bin collects half the photons (another factor 2): 16 times the variance at the same dose, or 16 times the dose for the same noise.

Math questions

B7. (math) Derive FBP: write f(\mathbf x) = \iint F(\boldsymbol\omega)e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf x}d\boldsymbol\omega in polar coordinates \boldsymbol\omega = \omega\mathbf n_\theta with \omega \in \mathbb R, \theta \in [0, \pi), and use the Fourier slice theorem.

B8. (math) Derive the Ram–Lak kernel: h[k] = \int_{-1/2}^{1/2}|f|\,e^{2\pi ifk}\,df.

B9. (math) For the untapered ramp with cutoff \omega_c, compute \int_{-\omega_c}^{\omega_c}|\omega|^2\,d\omega. For the Hann taper W(\omega) = \tfrac12(1 + \cos(\pi\omega/\omega_c)), show that the integral is still proportional to \omega_c^3.

B10. (math) Derive the angle rule: the detector has N bins (spacing 1), so the spectrum is sampled out to |\omega| = \tfrac12 in steps of 1/N. Require the arc between neighbouring spokes at the outer ring to be at most one step.

Solutions (math)

B7. d\boldsymbol\omega = |\omega|\,d\omega\,d\theta (each point of the plane is covered once by \omega \in \mathbb R, \theta \in [0, \pi)). So f(\mathbf x) = \int_0^\pi\int F(\omega\mathbf n_\theta)|\omega|e^{2\pi i\omega\,\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf n_\theta}d\omega\,d\theta. By the slice theorem F(\omega\mathbf n_\theta) = P_\theta(\omega), so the inner integral is the inverse 1D Fourier transform of |\omega|P_\theta(\omega) evaluated at \rho = \mathbf x\cdot\mathbf n_\theta: the ramp-filtered projection q_\theta(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf n_\theta). The outer integral is back-projection.

B8. |f| is even, so h[k] = 2\int_0^{1/2}f\cos(2\pi fk)\,df. For k = 0: 2 \cdot \tfrac18 = \tfrac14. For k \ne 0, integrate by parts: \int_0^{1/2}f\cos(af)df = \big[\tfrac{f\sin af}{a} + \tfrac{\cos af}{a^2}\big]_0^{1/2} with a = 2\pi k; \sin(\pi k) = 0, so h[k] = 2\,\frac{\cos(\pi k) - 1}{4\pi^2k^2} = \frac{(-1)^k - 1}{2\pi^2k^2}: -1/(\pi^2k^2) for odd k, 0 for even.

B9. \int_{-\omega_c}^{\omega_c}\omega^2d\omega = \tfrac23\omega_c^3. With \omega = \omega_c u: \int_{-\omega_c}^{\omega_c}\omega^2W(\omega)^2d\omega = \omega_c^3\int_{-1}^1 u^2\,\tfrac14(1 + \cos\pi u)^2du, a fixed number times \omega_c^3. Any taper that only scales with the cutoff gives the same cube law.

B10. Neighbouring spokes differ by \pi/N_\theta in angle; at radius \tfrac12 the arc between them is \tfrac{\pi}{2N_\theta}. Requiring \tfrac{\pi}{2N_\theta} \le \tfrac1N gives N_\theta \ge \tfrac{\pi}{2}N.

Solutions

E1. The filtered projection is a sharp positive peak with negative dips on both sides. One filtered smear is a bright stripe with dark (negative) borders. At the point all stripes add; around it the dark borders of each stripe cancel the bright tails of the others. With 180 angles the halo is gone: a clean point (RMSE 0.002). With 16 angles some star-shaped streaks remain far from the point.

E2. Ramp: RMSE 0.039, flat region mean 0.200 (the true value) with std 0.003. With none (plain back-projection) the image glows and its values are on a different scale (flat region mean 62.6): no RMSE is given.

E3. Noise std: ramp 0.048, Shepp–Logan 0.038, cosine 0.023, Hann 0.016. The ramp keeps all high frequencies; the Shepp–Logan taper lowers them a little, the cosine more, and Hann goes smoothly to 0 at the cutoff. The more a filter suppresses the high frequencies, where the noise is, the smoother the image, and the softer its edges (RMSE: ramp 0.059, Hann 0.075).

E4. 10^3: 0.048, 10^4: 0.017, 10^5: 0.005. Each factor 10 in dose divides the noise by about 3 (\sqrt{10} = 3.2): shot noise.

E5. Noise falls from 0.016 to 0.005 (ratio 3.2; the cube law predicts \sqrt8 = 2.8), but the RMSE rises from 0.075 to 0.116: the image is smooth but visibly blurred, the small ellipses smear out.

E6. Without padding the disc gets darker towards its rim (about 0.79 instead of 1 near the edge; the centre 0.98). The FFT filter is circular: the kernel’s negative tail reaching beyond one end of the projection wraps around to the other end, where the disc’s projection is large, and subtracts there. With zero padding the projection has room and the disc is flat.

Solutions

E7. 200 angles: RMSE 0.074 (most of it is the Hann filter’s softening of the skull edge). 18 angles: 0.109, with streaks radiating from the bright skull out into the background. In the spectrum only 18 spokes are drawn; far from the centre the gaps between them are many frequency steps wide. Streaks become visible somewhere below about 90 angles and obvious below 45, although the RMSE barely changes until then (0.074 at 90, 0.075 at 45): RMSE is not a good judge of streaks.

E8. The empty sector is around the vertical axis of the spectrum (the beam direction, vertical at 0° tilt), 60° wide. The phantom and the disc are stretched vertically: the disc (diameter 61 pixels) comes out about 58 wide and 70 tall, with blurred top and bottom edges. RMSE 0.132 for the phantom, 0.233 for the disc.

E9. 128 bins: 0.074; 64: 0.126; 32: 0.164. Wide bins average the projection over 2 or 4 pixels: fine detail is lost before reconstruction, so the image is blurred. For 32 bins the angle rule asks only for about 51 angles: with fewer bins, fewer angles suffice.

E10. 180 views with 10^3 photons each: RMSE 0.075; 30 views with 6 \times 10^3 each: 0.083; 15 views with 1.2 \times 10^4: 0.128. With FBP, spreading the photons over many views wins: noise falls with the total number of photons either way, but streaks from too few views do not go away with more photons per view.

Where this leads

FBP needs many angles and enough photons, and it cannot use anything we know about the object. The next unit writes reconstruction as a linear system A\mathbf x = \mathbf b, solves it iteratively (ART, SIRT), and adds priors (non-negativity, total variation), which is how low-dose CT and electron tomography cope with few views and missing wedges.
