Exercise: from PCA to autoencoders

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • treat a (small) image as a vector, and use PCA as an encoder and a decoder,
  • compute a reconstruction error, and say which images are rebuilt perfectly and which as the mean image,
  • explain why the bottleneck must be narrow,
  • explain why a curved family of images needs a nonlinear decoder.

The questions under By hand and Explore are for everyone. The Math questions (derive, prove) are for students who want the full treatment.

Background

Word Meaning
Image as a vector the grey values, one after the other: a 28\times28 image is a point in \mathbb R^{784}
Mean image \bar{\mathbf x} the pixel-wise average of all images
Principal component \mathbf u_k a unit vector (itself an image, an “eigen-image”); \mathbf u_1 points along the largest spread
Encode (PCA) scores s_k = \mathbf u_k \cdot (\mathbf x - \bar{\mathbf x})
Decode (PCA) \hat{\mathbf x} = \bar{\mathbf x} + s_1\mathbf u_1 + \dots + s_k\mathbf u_k
Reconstruction error \|\mathbf x - \hat{\mathbf x}\|^2, the squared length of what is lost
Autoencoder encoder and decoder are neural networks; trained so that \hat{\mathbf x} \approx \mathbf x

By hand

Part A — PCA as an encoder and a decoder

Two-pixel images, mean already subtracted. PC1 is \mathbf u = (0.8,\ 0.6), a unit vector.

B1. Encode \mathbf x = (3,\ 1): compute its score. Decode it again. What is the reconstruction error vector, and its squared length?

B2. Do the same for \mathbf y = (-1.6,\ -1.2) and \mathbf w = (-0.6,\ 0.8). Which one is rebuilt perfectly, and which one is rebuilt as the mean image (0, 0)? Explain both with the PC1 line.

B3. Now keep two components, with PC2 = (-0.6,\ 0.8). What is the reconstruction error of \mathbf x now? What does that say about an autoencoder whose bottleneck is as wide as its input?

Part B — brightness and edges

Two neighbouring pixels of a smooth image. The mean image is (0.5,\ 0.5), PC1 is \mathbf u_1 = (1, 1)/\sqrt2 and PC2 is \mathbf u_2 = (1, -1)/\sqrt2.

B4. Describe in words what \mathbf u_1 and \mathbf u_2 look like as two-pixel images, and what their scores measure.

B5. Encode the image (0.8,\ 0.6) with one component and decode it. What is lost? Give the error. What is the score on PC2?

B6. A 28\times28 image is encoded by PCA with 50 components. How many numbers are stored per image? What does the decoder add up to rebuild the image?

Part C — curved families

B7. A bright spot moves past two pixels (the lecture’s horseshoe). Explain in your own words why one PCA score cannot tell all spot positions apart, while one autoencoder number can.

B8. An autoencoder with no ReLU in its networks is trained on images. What will it find? (Use the collapse argument of Neural networks.)

Solutions

B1. Score = 0.8 \cdot 3 + 0.6 \cdot 1 = 3.0. Decoded 3.0 \cdot (0.8, 0.6) = (2.4,\ 1.8). Error (3, 1) - (2.4, 1.8) = (0.6,\ -0.8), squared length 0.36 + 0.64 = 1.0.

B2. \mathbf y: score = -1.28 - 0.72 = -2.0, decoded (-1.6,\ -1.2) = \mathbf y: error 0. \mathbf y lies on the PC1 line, so nothing is lost. \mathbf w: score = -0.48 + 0.48 = 0, decoded (0, 0), the mean image; error 0.36 + 0.64 = 1.0. \mathbf w is perpendicular to PC1: its one number carries no information about it.

B3. Scores 3.0 and -0.6 \cdot 3 + 0.8 \cdot 1 = -1.0. Decoded 3.0\,(0.8, 0.6) - 1.0\,(-0.6, 0.8) = (2.4 + 0.6,\ 1.8 - 0.8) = (3, 1): error 0. With as many numbers as pixels, encoding is a rotation and decoding rotates back. Nothing is lost, but nothing is summarised either: the bottleneck has to be narrower than the input to be useful.

B4. \mathbf u_1: both pixels equally bright, the score measures the overall brightness of the pair. \mathbf u_2: one pixel brighter, the other darker by the same amount, the score measures the difference, a tiny edge.

B5. \mathbf x - \bar{\mathbf x} = (0.3,\ 0.1). Score s_1 = (0.3 + 0.1)/\sqrt2 = 0.2828. Decoded \bar{\mathbf x} + s_1\mathbf u_1 = (0.5, 0.5) + (0.2, 0.2) = (0.7,\ 0.7). Error (0.1,\ -0.1), squared length 0.02. The edge between the two pixels is lost; its score on PC2 is (0.3 - 0.1)/\sqrt2 = 0.1414, and 0.1414^2 = 0.02 is exactly the lost error.

B6. 50 scores per image instead of 784 grey values. The decoder adds the mean image and 50 eigen-images, each multiplied by its score.

B7. One PCA score is a position along one straight line. The images of the moving spot lie on a bent curve, so when the curve is projected onto a line it folds: two different spot positions (for example -0.5 and 0.2) project to almost the same score, and the decoder must give them the same image. An autoencoder’s decoder is a curve, not a line: one number says where along the curve the image is, and different positions get different numbers.

B8. Without bends, the encoder is one matrix and the decoder is one matrix (plus shifts): a linear autoencoder. Trained on squared error, the best it can do is PCA: its decoder spans the same directions as the top principal components.

Math questions

B9. (math) Let S be the covariance matrix of centred data \mathbf y_i and \mathbf u a unit vector. Show that the mean squared error of the one-number linear autoencoder along \mathbf u is \operatorname{tr}S - \mathbf u^\top S\mathbf u. For S = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, compute this error for \mathbf u = (1, 0) and for \mathbf u = (1, 1)/\sqrt2, and find the best \mathbf u.

Solutions (math)

B9. (math) For a unit \mathbf u, \mathbf y = (\mathbf u^\top\mathbf y)\mathbf u + \mathbf r with \mathbf r \perp \mathbf u, so by Pythagoras \|\mathbf r\|^2 = \|\mathbf y\|^2 - (\mathbf u^\top\mathbf y)^2. Averaging over the data: \frac1n\sum\|\mathbf y_i\|^2 = \operatorname{tr}S and \frac1n\sum(\mathbf u^\top\mathbf y_i)^2 = \mathbf u^\top S\mathbf u. For the given S: \operatorname{tr}S = 4. \mathbf u = (1, 0): \mathbf u^\top S\mathbf u = 2, error 2. \mathbf u = (1, 1)/\sqrt2: \mathbf u^\top S\mathbf u = (2 + 1 + 1 + 2)/2 = 3, error 1. The eigenvalues of S are 3 (eigenvector (1,1)/\sqrt2) and 1 (eigenvector (1,-1)/\sqrt2); \mathbf u^\top S\mathbf u \le 3 for every unit vector, so (1,1)/\sqrt2 is best, with error equal to the dropped eigenvalue, 1.

Explore

Each dot is a two-pixel image (pixel 1 across, pixel 2 up). The blue line through the mean is a linear autoencoder that keeps one number: drag the handle u to turn it. Red segments join each image to its reconstruction. The small plot shows the mean squared error for every angle of \mathbf u; PCA jumps to the best angle. Click a dot to see that image and its reconstructions as two grey squares. On moving spot, train nonlinear autoencoder trains a 2 \to 16 \to 1 \to 16 \to 2 network (fixed seed) and draws its decoder curve in green.

E1. At the start, \mathbf u points along pixel 1 (angle 0°). What does this encoder keep, and what does the decoder do with pixel 2? Read the error. Check it against the readout’s “keep nothing” error and the covariance in the lecture’s math slide, if you did it.

E2. Turn \mathbf u slowly through all angles. At which angle is the error smallest, and at which largest? Press PCA to check. What do the two directions mean for a pair of neighbouring pixels?

E3. With PCA selected, click a few dots. Compare the image and its linear reconstruction as grey squares: what is always lost?

E4. Choose moving spot, then spot: PCA. Where are the red segments longest? Find two dots far apart along the curve that land on almost the same point of the line.

E5. Press train nonlinear autoencoder. Watch the green curve during training. Compare its final error with the PCA error. Why is the curve still one-dimensional (one number per image)?

Solutions

E1. At 0° the score is pixel 1 minus its mean: the encoder keeps pixel 1 and throws pixel 2 away. The decoder rebuilds pixel 1 exactly and sets pixel 2 to its mean, 0.511 (for the selected image (0.439, 0.466) it gives (0.439, 0.511)). Error 0.0414, half of the “keep nothing” error 0.0820. In the math: \operatorname{tr}S - S_{11} = 0.0820 - 0.0406 = 0.0414.

E2. Smallest at 45.3° (error 0.0058, the PCA direction): both pixels together, the brightness. Largest at −45° (0.0762): keeping only the difference of the two pixels and losing the brightness. Neighbouring pixels mostly differ in overall brightness from pair to pair, much less from each other.

E3. The reconstruction always has two (almost) equally bright pixels: the small difference between them, the local edge, is lost. Images that already have equal pixels are rebuilt almost perfectly.

E4. PCA error 0.0170. The red segments are longest at the two ends of the horseshoe (spot far left or far right, both pixels fairly dark), where the curve bends back towards the mean and is furthest from the line. The images at the start of the curve (spot far left, pixel 1 medium, pixel 2 dark) and those a little past pixel 1 (pixel 1 bright, pixel 2 a bit brighter) project to nearby points of the line: the line folds the curve.

E5. After 1500 steps the error is 0.0006, against 0.0170 for PCA: almost thirty times smaller. The green curve starts roughly straight and bends during training until it follows the horseshoe. It is still one number per image: the decoder maps one number z to one point of the plane, so all its outputs lie on one curve; the bends of the ReLU units let that curve bend.

Where this leads

A plain autoencoder rebuilds its training images, but its latent space can have gaps: decode a point between training images and nothing guarantees a sensible image. The variational autoencoder (next unit) turns each image into a small cloud and keeps all clouds near one standard bell shape, so that the whole latent space decodes to plausible images and new images can be generated.
