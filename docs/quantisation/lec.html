Bits and levels

With b bits a pixel can take 2^b values, 0, 1, \dots, 2^b - 1:

bits b 1 2 8 10 12 14 16
levels 2^b 2 4 256 1024 4096 16 384 65 536
  • Spread over the same range (say black to white), the step between neighbouring levels is \Delta = \text{range}/(2^b - 1).
  • One more bit → twice as many levels → half the step.
  • 8 bits: the step is 1/255 of the range. 12 bits: 1/4095, a 16 times finer step.

Try it: quantise an image

Start on the ramp at 4 bits: 16 visible bands. Slide the bits down to 1 and up to 8; at 6 bits most people stop seeing bands on a projector. Point at the error image: a sawtooth, between −Δ/2 and +Δ/2, and the histogram is flat. Then “4 bits: banding” on the smooth blobs: contour lines appear that are not in the scene. Then “4 bits + dither”: the bands are gone, replaced by fine grain, and the histogram becomes a triangle. Then the micrograph at 2 bits with error diffusion: only 4 grey levels, but it still looks like the cell.

What the rounding error looks like

  • Rounding moves each value to the nearest level, so the error is between −Δ/2 and +Δ/2.
  • In a smooth region the error is a sawtooth: the image jumps in steps of \Delta where it should change gradually. The eye sees the jumps as contour lines: banding (posterisation).
  • In a busy or noisy region, the error looks like random noise, evenly spread between −Δ/2 and +Δ/2.
  • Its typical size is about 0.29\,\Delta (root mean square). The demo’s readout shows it: at 4 bits, RMS error 0.0192 of the range.
  • One more bit halves the error. In decibels: about 6 dB per bit.

The math: the error of rounding

Model the error e as uniform on [-\Delta/2, \Delta/2], with density 1/\Delta. Then

\mathbb E[e] = 0, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(e) = \int_{-\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2} e^2\,\frac{1}{\Delta}\,de = \frac{1}{\Delta}\left[\frac{e^3}{3}\right]_{-\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2} = \frac{\Delta^2}{12}.

So \text{RMS} = \Delta/\sqrt{12} \approx 0.289\,\Delta. Here the signal-to-noise ratio in decibels compares variances: \text{SNR} = 10\log_{10}(\sigma^2_\text{signal}/\sigma^2_\text{noise}) (SNR and PSNR in general: camera-noise unit). For a signal spread evenly over the full range R = (2^b - 1)\Delta, \sigma^2_\text{signal} = R^2/12, so

\text{SNR} = 10\log_{10}\,(2^b - 1)^2 \approx 20\,b\log_{10}2 \approx 6.02\,b \text{ dB}.

(For a full-scale sine wave, the textbook value is 6.02\,b + 1.76 dB.) The model holds when the signal changes by many steps across the image; on a perfectly flat region the error is one constant number, not noise.

Dithering: add noise before rounding

Rounding a smooth gradient gives bands, because many neighbouring pixels round the same way.

  • Random dither: add a little noise (up to ±Δ/2) to every pixel before rounding. A pixel 75 % of the way from one level to the next now rounds up 75 % of the time. On average, the grey value is right.
  • The bands turn into fine grain. The total error is a bit larger (a triangle instead of a box, RMS \times\sqrt2), but it no longer follows the image, and the eye averages grain away.
  • Error diffusion (Floyd–Steinberg): round a pixel, then pass its error on to the neighbours not yet visited. The local average stays right. This is how a printer shows grey with only black ink.

The math: why random dither is unbiased

Measure in units of \Delta, write a value as x = k + f with k an integer and 0 \le f < 1, and add u uniform on [-\tfrac12, \tfrac12). Then x + u rounds up to k + 1 exactly when u \ge \tfrac12 - f, which has probability f:

\mathbb E[\operatorname{round}(x + u)] = (1 - f)\,k + f\,(k + 1) = k + f = x.

No bias, for every x: the expected output is a straight line, not a staircase. The price: the error is 1 - f with probability f and -f with probability 1 - f, so \mathbb E[e^2] = f(1-f); averaged over f, \int_0^1 f(1-f)\,df = \tfrac16. So the error variance is \Delta^2/6, twice that of plain rounding.

Summary

  • b bits give 2^b levels; rounding to them makes an error of at most half a step (typically 0.29 steps): about 6 dB per bit.
  • In smooth regions the error shows as banding; dithering trades the bands for fine, unbiased noise.
