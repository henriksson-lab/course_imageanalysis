Exercise: quantisation and bit depth

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • compute the number of levels and the step for a given bit depth, and quantise values by hand,
  • predict where banding appears and explain what dithering changes,
  • judge from a camera’s full well, read noise and ADC bits how many bits carry information.

Do the pen-and-paper part first, then the interactive part. Questions marked (math) are for those who want the derivations; the concept questions do not depend on them.

By hand

B1. How many grey levels do a 10-bit and a 14-bit sensor have? By about what factor is the step of a 12-bit image finer than that of an 8-bit image, if both cover black to white?

B2. Quantise the row of values (as fractions of the full range) 0.10,\quad 0.22,\quad 0.37,\quad 0.55,\quad 0.81 to 2 bits, i.e. to the levels 0, \tfrac13, \tfrac23, 1. Give the codes k \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}, the stored values and the errors. What is the largest error that rounding can ever make here?

B3. A flat region has the value 0.25 everywhere and is quantised to 2 bits. (a) Without dither, what does every pixel become, and what is the error? (b) With random dither, a pixel rounds up to \tfrac13 with some probability and down to 0 otherwise. Predict that probability, and the average over many pixels.

B4. A camera has a full well of 15 000 electrons, a read noise of 3 electrons, and a 12-bit ADC whose range is matched to the full well. (a) What is the gain (electrons per ADU)? (b) What is the dynamic range, as a ratio, in dB (20\log_{10}) and in bits? (c) Is 12 bits enough? Compare one ADU with the read noise.

B5. One pixel collects on average 400 electrons, another 40 000. Give the shot noise and the SNR of each. You average 4 frames of the dim pixel: what happens to its SNR, and why?

Solutions

B1. 2^{10} = 1024 and 2^{14} = 16\,384 levels. The steps are 1/255 and 1/4095 of the range: the 12-bit step is 4095/255 \approx 16 times finer.

B2. Multiply by 3 and round: 0.30 \to 0, 0.66 \to 1, 1.11 \to 1, 1.65 \to 2, 2.43 \to 2. Stored values 0, \tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23. Errors (stored − true): -0.100, +0.113, -0.037, +0.117, -0.143. Rounding never moves a value by more than half a step, \Delta/2 = \tfrac16 \approx 0.167.

B3. (a) 0.25 \times 3 = 0.75 rounds to 1: every pixel becomes \tfrac13, an error of +0.083 everywhere. The whole region is too bright by the same amount: no average can fix that. (b) The value is 75 % of the way from level 0 to level 1, so a pixel rounds up with probability 0.75: three quarters of the pixels become \tfrac13, one quarter 0. The average is 0.75 \cdot \tfrac13 = 0.25, the true value.

B4. (a) g = 15\,000 / 4095 \approx 3.66 electrons per ADU. (b) 15\,000/3 = 5000 : 1, which is 20\log_{10}5000 \approx 74.0 dB, or \log_2 5000 \approx 12.3 bits. (c) Just about. One ADU (3.66 electrons) is a little larger than the read noise (3 electrons), so the quantisation noise 3.66/\sqrt{12} \approx 1.06 electrons is not negligible. Added in variance it raises the dark noise from 3 to \sqrt{3^2 + 1.06^2} \approx 3.18 electrons, about 6 % more. A 13-bit ADC would make one ADU smaller than the read noise.

B5. Shot noise \sqrt{400} = 20 and \sqrt{40\,000} = 200 electrons; SNR 400/20 = 20 and 40\,000/200 = 200. 100 times more light gives 10 times the SNR. Averaging 4 frames of the dim pixel is like collecting 4 times the light (1600 electrons, noise 40): the SNR doubles, to 40. Averaging only helps because the noise differs from frame to frame.

Math questions

B6. (math) Let the rounding error e be uniform on [-\Delta/2, \Delta/2]. Show that \operatorname{Var}(e) = \Delta^2/12. A signal spread evenly over the full range R = (2^b - 1)\Delta has variance R^2/12: show that the SNR is 20\log_{10}(2^b - 1) \approx 6.02\,b dB, and evaluate it for b = 4.

B7. (math) Random dither: in units of \Delta, x = k + f with 0 \le f < 1 and u uniform on [-\tfrac12, \tfrac12). Show that \mathbb E[\operatorname{round}(x + u)] = x, and that the mean squared error, averaged over f uniform on [0, 1), is \Delta^2/6. By what factor is the RMS error larger than without dither?

B8. (math) With shot noise, read noise \sigma_r and quantisation (gain g) independent, the total noise is \sigma^2 = S + \sigma_r^2 + g^2/12. For the camera of B4, compute \sigma at S = 100 electrons. Above which signal does shot noise dominate the read noise? Show that the best possible SNR is \sqrt{N_\text{max}} (ignore read and quantisation noise at the full well).

Solutions (math)

B6. \mathbb E[e] = 0 by symmetry, so \operatorname{Var}(e) = \int_{-\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2} e^2 \tfrac{1}{\Delta}\,de = \tfrac{1}{\Delta}\cdot\tfrac{2}{3}\left(\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\right)^3 = \tfrac{\Delta^2}{12}. Then \text{SNR} = 10\log_{10}\frac{R^2/12}{\Delta^2/12} = 20\log_{10}(2^b - 1) \approx 20\,b\log_{10}2 = 6.02\,b dB. For b = 4: 20\log_{10}15 \approx 23.5 dB (6.02 · 4 = 24.1 is the large-b approximation). The demo’s ramp at 4 bits shows exactly 23.5 dB.

B7. x + u reaches the rounding threshold k + \tfrac12 when u \ge \tfrac12 - f, with probability f; then the output is k + 1, otherwise k. So \mathbb E = (1-f)k + f(k+1) = x. The error is 1 - f (probability f) or -f (probability 1-f): \mathbb E[e^2] = f(1-f)^2 + (1-f)f^2 = f(1-f), and \int_0^1 f(1-f)\,df = \tfrac12 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac16. In units of \Delta^2: \Delta^2/6, twice \Delta^2/12, so the RMS error is \sqrt2 \approx 1.41 times larger. (The demo: 0.0272 vs 0.0192 on the ramp at 4 bits, a ratio of 1.42.)

B8. \sigma = \sqrt{100 + 9 + 1.06^2} \approx 10.5 electrons. Shot noise exceeds read noise when \sqrt S > \sigma_r, i.e. S > \sigma_r^2 = 9 electrons. At the full well the noise is \sqrt{N_\text{max}}, so \text{SNR} = N_\text{max}/\sqrt{N_\text{max}} = \sqrt{N_\text{max}}: about 122 for 15 000 electrons.

Explore

Quantising an image

The demo rounds an image to b bits and shows the rounding error (0 = grey, \pm\Delta = white/black) and its histogram. Images: a ramp, smooth blobs, and the 8-bit micrograph.

E1. On the ramp, step the bits from 8 down to 1. Compare the RMS error with \Delta/\sqrt{12} in the readout. How much does the SNR change per bit?

E2. Click 4 bits: banding, then 4 bits + dither. What happens to the contour lines, to the error histogram, and to the RMS error? Which picture looks closer to the original?

E3. Micrograph at 2 bits: compare no dither and error diffusion. Which has the larger RMS error? Which shows the cell better? What does that tell you about RMS error as a measure of image quality?

E4. Micrograph at 8 bits: the RMS error is 0. Why?

Solutions

E1. They agree to the 4th decimal at every bit depth (e.g. 0.0192 at 4 bits, 0.0046 at 6 bits): on a ramp the error is exactly uniform. The SNR goes 23.5, 29.8, 36.0 dB for 4, 5, 6 bits: about 6 dB per bit.

E2. The contour lines disappear and become fine grain. The histogram changes from a box on \pm\Delta/2 to a triangle on \pm\Delta, and the RMS error grows from 0.0192 to 0.0273, about \sqrt2 times. Yet the dithered image looks closer to the original from a distance: its error has no structure, and the eye averages it away.

E3. Error diffusion has the larger RMS error (0.1200 against 0.0968), but it shows far more of the cell: with only 4 grey levels, the local average of the diffused pixels follows the true grey value. The RMS error compares pixels one by one and ignores what the eye does, which is to average neighbours. A small number is not always a better picture.

E4. The micrograph is stored with 8 bits already, so every value is exactly one of the 256 levels and rounding changes nothing. Quantising can only lose information; it cannot add the bits that were never measured.

How many bits does a camera need?

A simulated camera images the micrograph. The plot shows noise against signal, in electrons: shot noise, read noise, quantisation noise, their total, and points measured on simulated flat patches.

E5. Preset sCMOS 16 bit. How large is one ADU in electrons, and the quantisation noise? Which noise source dominates in the dark, and which in the bright? Do the measured points follow the model?

E6. Same, 8-bit ADC. What happens to the gain and the quantisation noise? Why are the measured points for dark patches drawn as red triangles (measured noise 0)? Is zero noise good news here?

E7. Compare low light, 8 bit and low light, 12 bit: how many different ADU values does the image use, and what do you see?

E8. Noisy, 16-bit ADC: how many ADU is the read noise? How many bits does the readout say are needed? What do the other bits store?

Solutions

E5. One ADU is 30\,000/65\,535 \approx 0.46 electrons, and the quantisation noise 0.13 electrons, far below the read noise of 1.5. In the dark the read noise dominates (the flat blue line), above a few electrons the shot noise \sqrt S takes over. The points sit on the total curve (e.g. 9.89 measured against 10.11 predicted at 100 electrons). The last point, at the full well, falls below: saturated pixels are all equal, so they have no noise left.

E6. One ADU is now about 118 electrons and the quantisation noise 34 electrons, far above the read noise. In a dark flat patch the true noise (a few electrons) is much smaller than one ADU, so every pixel rounds to the same number: the measured spread is 0. That is not good news. The signal differences below one ADU are not noisy, they are gone. The model g/\sqrt{12} also fails at 1000 electrons (measured about 59, predicted 46): it assumes the signal varies over many steps.

E7. 8 bits: only 4 different values in the image (the brightest pixel is 2.6 ADU), so the cell is a few flat patches. 12 bits: 48 values (brightest 41 ADU); the cell is recognisable, and noisy. In dim light the ADC’s step, not the sensor, decides what survives.

E8. The read noise is 10 electrons and one ADU is 10\,000/65\,535 \approx 0.15 electrons, so the read noise is about 66 ADU, around 2^6. The readout says 10 bits would do (\log_2 1000 \approx 10). The lowest ~6 bits of every pixel record read noise very precisely.

Where this leads

The noise model here, Poisson shot noise plus Gaussian read noise, is the starting point of denoising and of choosing exposure times in quantitative microscopy. The unit on 12- and 16-bit images is about storing and displaying the numbers such cameras produce.
