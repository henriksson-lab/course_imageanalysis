Exercise: graph-cut segmentation

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • build the graph of a small image: t-links from the data term, n-links from the smoothness term,
  • check that the cost of a cut equals the energy of its labelling, and find the minimum,
  • compute contrast-sensitive weights and see how they change the result,
  • use seeds, and explain the shrinking bias and metrication.

Do the pen-and-paper part first, then the two demos.

Background

Idea What it means
Nodes one per pixel, plus s (object) and t (background)
t-links s \to p with capacity D_p(0), p \to t with capacity D_p(1)
n-links p \to q and q \to p, each with capacity \lambda w_{pq}, for 4-neighbours p, q
Labelling from a cut L_p = 1 (object) if p is on the source side, else 0
Cut = energy c(S, T) = \sum_p D_p(L_p) + \lambda \sum w_{pq}[L_p \ne L_q] = E(L)
Contrast weights w_{pq} = \exp\!\big(-(I_p - I_q)^2/2\sigma^2\big); \sigma = \infty means w_{pq} = 1
Seeds an object seed gets s \to p with infinite capacity; a background seed p \to t

Throughout, grey levels are 0 to 3 with \mu_0 = 0, \mu_1 = 3 and D_p(\ell) = (I_p - \mu_\ell)^2, so D_p(0) = I_p^2 and D_p(1) = (3 - I_p)^2. This is what the first demo uses.

By hand

B1. The 1 \times 3 image I = (3, 1, 3), with w_{pq} = 1. (a) Draw the graph: 5 nodes, 6 t-links, 2 pairs of n-links. Write every capacity for \lambda = 1. (b) List all 8 cuts (labellings) with their cost. Which is the minimum? Which labelling does thresholding give? (c) Repeat (b) for \lambda = 2. At which \lambda does the minimum change?

B2. Same image, now with contrast weights, \sigma = 1. (a) Compute w_{12} and w_{23}. What is w_{pq} for a grey-level difference of 1, and of 3? (b) Compute the energy of 101 and 111 for \lambda = 2 and \lambda = 5. Up to which \lambda does the dark pixel stay background? (c) Is that what you want if the dark pixel is noise? If it is a real gap between two objects?

B3. The 2 \times 2 image \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, 4-neighbours (4 pairs of n-links), w_{pq} = 1, \lambda = 2. Write labellings as top-left, top-right, bottom-left, bottom-right, e.g. 1100 = top row object. (a) Give the t-link capacities of the four pixels. (b) Compute the cost of the cuts 1100, 1000, 1110, 1111 and 0000. Then argue (or check by listing all 16) which cut is the minimum.

B4. Same 2 \times 2 image and \lambda, but the bottom-left pixel (grey level 1) is an object seed. Which capacities change? What is the minimum cut now, and its cost (counting the seed’s data cost D(1) = 4)?

B5. Shrinking bias. A 6 \times 6 image is 0 everywhere except column 2, which is a vertical line of grey level 2, and columns 4–6, a band of grey level 2 that touches the right border. Use w_{pq} = 1. (a) How much data cost does the line save by being object rather than background? How many n-links does labelling it object cut? Above which \lambda does the minimum drop the line? (b) The same for the band. (Its right side lies on the image border: no n-links there.) (c) Generalise: a long strip of width k pixels and data gain g per pixel. Above which \lambda is it removed?

B6. Metrication. Consider a long straight boundary in a large image with w_{pq} = 1. (a) With 4-neighbours, how many n-links does a horizontal boundary of length \ell cut? And a 45° staircase boundary of the same Euclidean length? (b) With 8-neighbours, horizontal and vertical n-links get weight \tfrac12, diagonal ones \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt2}. Compute the cost per unit length of a horizontal and of a 45° boundary. (c) Which neighbourhood gives the more “round” results, and why?

B7 (math). Show that the pairwise term V(L_p, L_q) = \lambda w_{pq}[L_p \ne L_q] with \lambda w_{pq} \ge 0 satisfies V(0,1) + V(1,0) \ge V(0,0) + V(1,1). Which pairwise term in a segmentation energy would violate it?

Solutions

B1. (a) D(0) = 9, 1, 9 and D(1) = 0, 4, 0: t-links s \to 1: 9, s \to 2: 1, s \to 3: 9; 1 \to t: 0, 2 \to t: 4, 3 \to t: 0. n-links 1 \leftrightarrow 2 and 2 \leftrightarrow 3: capacity \lambda = 1 each way. (b) Writing data + boundaries:

labelling data boundaries cost, \lambda = 1 cost, \lambda = 2
101 0 + 1 + 0 = 1 2 3 5
111 0 + 4 + 0 = 4 0 4 4
001, 100 10 1 11 12
011, 110 13 1 14 15
000 19 0 19 19
010 22 2 24 26

At \lambda = 1 the minimum is 101 (cost 3), the same as thresholding. (c) At \lambda = 2 the minimum is 111 (cost 4). 101 costs 1 + 2\lambda, 111 costs 4: they tie at \lambda = 1.5.

B2. (a) w_{12} = w_{23} = e^{-2^2/2} = e^{-2} = 0.135. For a difference of 1: e^{-0.5} = 0.607; of 3: e^{-4.5} = 0.011. (b) E(101) = 1 + \lambda \cdot 2 \cdot 0.135: 1.541 at \lambda = 2, 2.353 at \lambda = 5. E(111) = 4. The dark pixel stays background as long as 1 + 0.271\lambda < 4, i.e. up to \lambda = 11.08. (c) Contrast weights make a boundary across a large intensity jump almost free. That protects a real gap between two objects, but it also protects a noise pixel that differs strongly from its neighbours. \sigma should be larger than typical noise differences and smaller than the jumps at real edges.

B3. (a) Grey levels 3, 2, 1, 0: s \to p: 9, 4, 1, 0; p \to t: 0, 1, 4, 9. (b) 1100: data 0 + 1 + 1 + 0 = 2, n-links cut: top-left–bottom-left and top-right–bottom-right, 2, cost 2 + 2 \cdot 2 = 6. 1000: data 0 + 4 + 1 + 0 = 5, 2 n-links, cost 9. 1110: data 0 + 1 + 4 + 0 = 5, 2 n-links, cost 9. 1111: data 0 + 1 + 4 + 9 = 14, cost 14. 0000: data 9 + 4 + 1 + 0 = 14, cost 14. The minimum is 1100 with cost 6: it has the smallest possible data cost (each pixel takes its cheaper label, total 2), and any labelling with both labels cuts at least 2 n-links, so no labelling with a boundary can beat 2 + 4 = 6; the two without a boundary cost 14. Listing all 16 confirms it; the next best are 1000 and 1110 (9).

B4. The seed’s t-links become s \to p: \infty, p \to t: 0 (and its data cost D(1) = 4 is paid in any allowed labelling). Only labellings with bottom-left = 1 are allowed. The best is 1110: data 0 + 1 + 4 + 0 = 5, 2 n-links (top-right–bottom-right and bottom-left–bottom-right), cost 5 + 4 = 9. Next: 1010 (12) and 1111 (14). The seed pulls its pixel into the object, and the boundary moves to the right of it.

B5. (a) Each line pixel saves D(0) - D(1) = 4 - 1 = 3; 6 pixels save 18. As object, the line cuts 6 n-links on each side, 12 in total (top and bottom are the image border): cost 12\lambda. Dropped when 12\lambda > 18, i.e. \lambda > 1.5. (b) The band: 18 pixels save 54; it cuts only the 6 n-links on its left side: dropped when 6\lambda > 54, i.e. \lambda > 9. Same intensity, 6 times more robust. (c) Per unit length, a strip of width k gains kg and its two sides cost 2\lambda: it is removed for \lambda > kg/2. Thin structures go first.

B6. (a) Horizontal: \ell vertical n-links, cost \ell, i.e. 1 per unit length. A 45° staircase of Euclidean length \ell goes \ell/\sqrt2 steps right and \ell/\sqrt2 down and cuts one n-link per step: \sqrt2\,\ell, i.e. 1.41 per unit length. (b) Horizontal: per unit length, one vertical n-link (\tfrac12) and two diagonal ones (2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt2}): \tfrac12 + \tfrac1{\sqrt2} = 1.207. At 45°, per step of length \sqrt2: one horizontal and one vertical n-link (\tfrac12 + \tfrac12) and two diagonals across the boundary (\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}; the diagonals along the boundary are not cut): (1 + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})/\sqrt2 = 1.207 per unit length. The same. (c) 8-neighbours: the boundary cost no longer depends on direction (at least for these two directions), so the cut has no reason to prefer axis-aligned edges. With 4-neighbours, diagonal boundaries are 41% more expensive and shapes come out blocky.

B7. V(0,1) + V(1,0) = 2\lambda w_{pq} \ge 0 = V(0,0) + V(1,1). A term that rewards disagreement, V(0,1) < 0 (or that charges more for equal labels than for different ones), violates it; for example a term that wants neighbouring pixels to differ, as in a checkerboard pattern.

Explore: the graph of a small image

The demo draws a small image as its graph. Pixels show their grey level (0–3) and are framed orange (object) or blue (background) according to the minimum cut. Small images (1 × 3, 2 × 2) show the whole graph with every capacity, and the cut edges dashed red. The 6 × 6 images show the t-links as two bars inside each pixel (left: s \to p, right: p \to t; the cut one is solid), and n-links as lines whose width is \lambda w_{pq}. Click a pixel to change its grey level, or choose label (your cut) and click pixels to make your own labelling; the table then compares its energy with the minimum.

P1. Check B1 and B2 in the demo: the 1 × 3 image at \lambda = 1 and \lambda = 2, then \sigma = 1 at \lambda = 2. Do the cut costs and the max flow match your table?

P2. Choose 2 × 2 (\lambda = 2) and label (your cut). Make the labellings of B3(b) one by one and compare their costs with yours.

P3. Choose 6 × 6 blob (\lambda = 1.5). Which pixels does the minimum cut label differently from thresholding (set \lambda = 0 to see thresholding)? Check that E = data + \lambda \cdot \Sigma w in the table.

P4. Choose thin vs thick and raise \lambda. At which \lambda does the line disappear, and at which the band? Compare with B5.

P5. Choose noisy edge (\lambda = 1, \sigma = 1). One noise pixel in the dark half is labelled object. Why does the cut keep it? Set \sigma = \infty: what happens to it, and to the energy?

Solutions

P1. At \lambda = 1, \sigma = \infty: labelling 101 with cost 3 (data 1, \Sigma w = 2), max flow 3. At \lambda = 2: 111, cost 4. With \sigma = 1 at \lambda = 2: the n-links around the middle pixel shrink to 0.27 each (\lambda w = 2 \cdot 0.135), and 101 is the minimum again with cost 1.54 (shown as data 1, \Sigma w 0.27, \lambda \Sigma w 0.54).

P2. 1100: 6 (the minimum, the demo’s own cut), 1000 and 1110: 9, 1111 and 0000: 14. Every cut you make has cost = energy, and none is below 6.

P3. Thresholding (\lambda = 0) labels 13 pixels object with energy 5: it leaves the dark pixel (grey level 1) inside the blob as background and takes the two isolated pixels of grey level 2 (right of the blob and at the right edge) as object. At \lambda = 1.5 the minimum cut fills the hole and drops both isolated pixels: 12 object pixels, the block of 4 rows and 3 columns. The table shows data 14, \Sigma w 14, E = 14 + 1.5 \cdot 14 = 35 = max flow.

P4. The line disappears at \lambda = 1.5 (at exactly 1.5 the two labellings tie, and the solver drops the line), the band at \lambda = 9 (again a tie). As computed in B5: the band has 9 times the data gain per row but only half the boundary.

P5. The pixel (row 4, column 2, grey level 2) has dark neighbours (0), so with \sigma = 1 each of its 4 n-links has weight e^{-2} = 0.135: isolating it costs only 4 \cdot 0.135 = 0.54, less than the data it saves (4 - 1 = 3). The contrast weights protect it as if it were a real edge. With \sigma = \infty all weights are 1, isolating it costs 4, and the cut drops it; the energy rises from 12.5 to 23, because every boundary is now expensive, including the real one between the halves.

Explore: interactive segmentation

A 128 \times 128 image: a synthetic test image (a bright disk with a thin arm, with noise) or a micrograph of a cell. Paint seeds with the mouse: object (red) or background (blue); erase and clear remove them. The intensity model is estimated from the seeds: a Gaussian per label, or a histogram. The large image shows the graph-cut result (orange, with a yellow boundary), the small one thresholding with the same model (\lambda = 0). The table gives the energy of both, the number of object pixels, and, for the test image, the number of wrong pixels compared with the truth. 4 / 8 chooses the neighbourhood; \sigma the contrast weights (\infty: none).

G1. Preset noise (\lambda = 1.5). Compare thresholding and graph cut: wrong pixels, data term and total energy. Which has the smaller data term? Check that the max flow equals E.

G2. Still on noise, move \lambda. Which \lambda gives the fewest wrong pixels? At which \lambda does part of the thin arm disappear, and when is it almost completely gone? Explain with B5.

G3. Preset thin arm lost (\lambda = 6). Now set \sigma = 50. What happens to the arm and to the number of wrong pixels? Why?

G4. Preset blocky (4 neighbours). Describe the shape of the disk. Switch to 8 neighbours. What changes, and what happens to the number of wrong pixels?

G5. Preset nucleus (micrograph, histograms, \lambda = 10, \sigma = 25). Where does the segmentation leak outside the nucleus? Paint a background seed there and see the result update. Then set \sigma = \infty: how does the boundary change?

G6. Preset overlapping intensities: the same object seeds, background seeds only on the white areas. What is segmented, and why? Fix it with a few background strokes on the grey cytoplasm around the nucleus.

Solutions

G1. Thresholding: 2587 wrong pixels, graph cut: 55. The data term of thresholding is smaller (69111.6 against 71491.9): by definition it is the smallest possible data term. But its boundary is enormous (\Sigma w = 7351.0 against 335.6), so its total energy at \lambda = 1.5 is 80138.2, against 71995.3 for the cut. The max flow is 71995.3 = E. (The large numbers come from the constant \log s in the Gaussian data term, the same for every labelling.)

G2. The fewest wrong pixels are at \lambda = 1.5 (55); from \lambda \approx 1 to 1.6 it stays between about 55 and 110. At \lambda = 1.7 the outer half of the arm disappears (wrong pixels jump to 102), at \lambda = 2.75 most of it (137), and from \lambda = 4.7 only a stub of a few pixels is left. The arm is 3 pixels wide: as in B5, its data gain per unit length is about 3g while its two long sides cost 2\lambda (times the 8-neighbour weight 1.21), so it goes long before the disk is affected.

G3. With \sigma = 50 the arm comes back (almost all of it), but the number of wrong pixels rises from 138 to 247. The n-links across the arm’s edges now have small weights (the jump from 80 to 170 is large compared with \sigma), so cutting along the arm is cheap. But noise also produces large differences between neighbours, so noisy pixels are cheaper to cut out as well: more specks survive. Contrast weights help only when edges are larger than the noise.

G4. With 4 neighbours (noise 90, \lambda = 6) the disk comes out as a rounded square with flat horizontal and vertical sides: 457 wrong pixels. With 8 neighbours it is round again and the error falls to 217. With 4 neighbours a diagonal boundary costs 41% more per unit length (B6), so the cut trades the diagonal parts of the circle for axis-aligned ones.

G5. With these seeds the nucleus is found along its envelope (3152 object pixels), with a leak into dark material at the right edge of the image. A background stroke there removes it. With \sigma = \infty (no contrast weights) the boundary no longer follows the dark nuclear envelope: at \lambda = 10 it takes short cuts and the object shrinks to 1779 pixels. The contrast weights make the envelope the cheapest place for the boundary.

G6. The whole cell and its neighbours are segmented (6239 object pixels): the cytoplasm and the other dark material are included. The background model has only seen white, so every grey pixel looks more like the object than like the background; nucleus and cytoplasm have overlapping grey levels, and the data term cannot tell them apart. A few background strokes on the cytoplasm add its grey levels to the background model; with the contrast weights at the envelope the result approaches the nucleus preset.

Where this leads

Graph cuts need seeds or a given intensity model, and they find the exact best labelling of a restricted energy. The next unit drops the seeds: normalized cuts look for a balanced split of the whole graph, using an eigenvector of the graph Laplacian.
