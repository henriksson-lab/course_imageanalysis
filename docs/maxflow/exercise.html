Exercise: graphs, cuts and maximum flow

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • compute the cost of an s–t cut,
  • find a maximum flow by hand with augmenting paths, using the residual graph,
  • read a minimum cut off the final residual graph, and check that flow = cut,
  • explain why backward arcs are needed and which edges limit the flow.

Do the pen-and-paper part first, then the interactive part.

Background

Idea What it means
Capacity c(u, v) \ge 0 on every directed edge u \to v
s–t cut nodes split into S \ni s and T \ni t; cost c(S, T) = sum of capacities of edges from S to T (edges from T to S do not count)
Flow 0 \le f(u, v) \le c(u, v), and flow in = flow out at every node except s and t
Residual graph forward arc u \to v with capacity c - f; backward arc v \to u with capacity f
Augmenting path a path from s to t in the residual graph; push its smallest residual capacity (the bottleneck)
Max-flow = min-cut at the end, S = nodes reachable from s in the residual graph is a minimum cut, and its cost equals the flow

By hand

The graph for B1–B4 has nodes s, a, b, c, d, t and edges

edge s\!\to\!a s\!\to\!b a\!\to\!b a\!\to\!c b\!\to\!d c\!\to\!d c\!\to\!t d\!\to\!t
capacity 8 4 8 5 4 7 4 7

Draw it first: s on the left, a and c on top, b and d below, t on the right.

B1. Compute the cost of the cuts S = \{s\}, \{s, a\}, \{s, b\}, \{s, a, b\} and \{s, a, b, c, d\}. Which is the smallest of these five? What does that tell you about the maximum flow?

B2. Find a maximum flow with augmenting paths. Always take a shortest path (fewest edges) in the residual graph. For each step, write the path, its bottleneck and the total flow so far. Give the final flow on every edge.

B3. Check the flow of B2: is the capacity constraint respected on every edge, and is flow in = flow out at a, b, c and d?

B4. Draw the final residual graph. Which nodes are reachable from s? Which edges cross from this set to the rest, and what is the cost of this cut? Compare with B1 and B2.

B5. Which single edge’s capacity would you raise to increase the maximum flow? What is the maximum flow if you raise c(a, c) from 5 to 6? From 5 to 8? Why does raising c(c, t) not help?

B6. Four nodes s, a, b, t, all capacities 1: s\!\to\!a, s\!\to\!b, a\!\to\!b, a\!\to\!t, b\!\to\!t. (a) What is the maximum flow? Give a minimum cut. (b) Suppose the first augmenting path is s \to a \to b \to t. Draw the residual graph. Is there a path from s to t using forward arcs only? Using backward arcs too? Push along it and give the final flow.

Solutions

B1. \{s\}: s\!\to\!a + s\!\to\!b = 8 + 4 = 12. \{s, a\}: s\!\to\!b + a\!\to\!b + a\!\to\!c = 4 + 8 + 5 = 17. \{s, b\}: s\!\to\!a + b\!\to\!d = 8 + 4 = 12. \{s, a, b\}: a\!\to\!c + b\!\to\!d = 5 + 4 = 9. \{s, a, b, c, d\}: c\!\to\!t + d\!\to\!t = 4 + 7 = 11. The smallest is \{s, a, b\} with 9, so the maximum flow is at most 9.

B2. There are two shortest paths (3 edges): s \to a \to c \to t, bottleneck \min(8, 5, 4) = 4, flow 4; and s \to b \to d \to t, bottleneck \min(4, 4, 7) = 4, flow 8 (either order). Now s\!\to\!b, b\!\to\!d and c\!\to\!t are full. The next shortest path is s \to a \to c \to d \to t, bottleneck \min(8 - 4, 5 - 4, 7, 7 - 4) = 1: flow 9. Then a\!\to\!c is full as well, and no path is left. Final flow: s\!\to\!a 5, s\!\to\!b 4, a\!\to\!b 0, a\!\to\!c 5, b\!\to\!d 4, c\!\to\!d 1, c\!\to\!t 4, d\!\to\!t 5.

B3. Every flow is between 0 and the capacity. At a: in 5 (s\!\to\!a), out 0 + 5 (a\!\to\!b, a\!\to\!c). At b: in 4 + 0, out 4. At c: in 5, out 1 + 4. At d: in 4 + 1, out 5. Flow out of s: 5 + 4 = 9 = flow into t: 4 + 5 = 9.

B4. Residual arcs leaving s: s\!\to\!a (3 left); s\!\to\!b is full. From a: a\!\to\!b (8 left), a\!\to\!c is full, backward a\!\to\!s. From b: b\!\to\!d is full; only backward arcs to s (and none to a, since a\!\to\!b carries no flow). So the reachable set is S = \{s, a, b\}. Edges from S to T = \{c, d, t\}: a\!\to\!c (5) and b\!\to\!d (4), cost 9, both full. Flow 9 = cut 9: this is the minimum cut of B1.

B5. Only raising an edge of the minimum cut can help: a\!\to\!c or b\!\to\!d. With c(a, c) = 6 the cut \{s, a, b\} costs 10 and that is the new maximum flow (all other cuts cost at least 11). With c(a, c) = 8 the cut \{s, a, b\} costs 12, but now \{s, a, b, c, d\} (11) is the smallest: maximum flow 11. c\!\to\!t is not in the minimum cut, so raising it leaves the cut of 9, and the flow, unchanged.

B6. (a) Maximum flow 2, for example s \to a \to t and s \to b \to t. A minimum cut: S = \{s\}, cost 1 + 1 = 2 (also \{s, b\}: s\!\to\!a + b\!\to\!t = 2). (b) After pushing 1 along s \to a \to b \to t, the arcs s\!\to\!a, a\!\to\!b, b\!\to\!t are full. Forward arcs with room: s\!\to\!b and a\!\to\!t only; from s we reach b, and from b no forward arc leads anywhere. Stuck at 1. With backward arcs: b \to a (capacity 1, undoing the flow on a\!\to\!b). Path s \to b \to a \to t, bottleneck 1. Pushing it sets s\!\to\!b = 1, a\!\to\!b = 0, a\!\to\!t = 1. Final flow: s\!\to\!a 1, s\!\to\!b 1, a\!\to\!b 0, a\!\to\!t 1, b\!\to\!t 1: value 2.

The math: why no flow exceeds any cut

For any cut (S, T), the value of a flow equals the flow from S to T minus the flow from T to S. For a node u \ne s in S, flow out minus flow in is 0 (conservation); for s it is |f|. Add these over all u \in S: |f| = \sum_{u \in S} \big(\sum_v f(u,v) - \sum_v f(v,u)\big). An edge with both ends in S appears once with + and once with - and cancels. What is left are the edges leaving S (with +) and the edges entering S from T (with -): |f| = \sum_{S\to T} f - \sum_{T \to S} f. Since f \le c on the first sum and f \ge 0 on the second, |f| \le \sum_{S \to T} c = c(S, T).

Explore

The demo shows a small graph with flow / capacity on every edge. Next augmenting path finds a shortest path from s to t in the residual graph (orange) and pushes its bottleneck; run to end does all steps; flow = 0 starts again. Show the residual graph draws the residual arcs instead: grey for capacity left, red for flow that can be undone. When no path is left, the nodes reachable from s are shaded blue, the rest orange, and the edges of the cut are red and dashed. Cut nearest t shows the other extreme minimum cut. Click an edge to change its capacity.

E1. With the example, step through with next augmenting path. Write down each path and its bottleneck. When no path is left, which nodes are on the source side, and which edges form the cut? Edge a \to b goes from the sink side to the source side: why does it not count, and what flow does it carry?

E2. Step through again with show the residual graph ticked. After the second step, which arcs leave s? Why is there a red arc from t to d?

E3. Choose needs a back-edge and step through. Which edge does the second path use backwards, and what happens to the flow on that edge? What would happen without backward arcs?

E4. Choose two minimum cuts and run to end. Which cut does the demo show? Tick cut nearest t: which cut is it now, and what does it cost? Now raise c(c, t) from 4 to 5: does the maximum flow change? Then reset and raise c(s, b) from 2 to 3 instead: what changes?

E5. Back to the example. Raise c(b, d) from 9 to 12. What is the new maximum flow, and where is the new minimum cut? Reset and raise c(c, t) to 12 instead. Explain the difference.

Solutions

E1. Paths: s\,a\,c\,t (bottleneck 4), s\,a\,d\,t (6), s\,b\,d\,t (4), s\,b\,d\,c\,t (5): total 19. The source side is S = \{s, b\}; the cut edges are s\!\to\!a (10) and b\!\to\!d (9): 19 = flow. a\!\to\!b goes from T to S, so it is not part of the cost; at the end it carries 0 flow, as the max-flow / min-cut argument requires (if it carried flow, its backward arc b \to a would make a reachable from s).

E2. After two steps (s\,a\,c\,t with 4 and s\,a\,d\,t with 6), s\!\to\!a is full: from s there is only the forward arc s \to b (10 left); s\!\to\!a now appears only as a red backward arc a \to s with capacity 10. The red arc t \to d (capacity 6) is the backward arc of d\!\to\!t, which carries 6: flow that could be sent back.

E3. The second path is s\,c\,g\,b\,a\,d\,e\,t. It uses a \to b backwards (as b \to a): the 4 units the first path sent along a \to b are cancelled, and the flow on a\!\to\!b drops to 0. In effect the first path is rerouted to s\,a\,d\,e\,t, and the second path takes over b\!\to\!t. Without backward arcs, after the first path (s\,a\,b\,t), b\!\to\!t and s\!\to\!a are full and t cannot be reached from s: the algorithm would stop at 4 instead of 8.

E4. The demo shows S = \{s\}: cut s\!\to\!a + s\!\to\!b = 5 + 2 = 7. Cut nearest t: S = \{s, a, b, c, d\}, cut c\!\to\!t + d\!\to\!t = 4 + 3 = 7. Raising c(c, t) to 5: the flow stays 7, because the cut \{s\} still costs 7. Raising c(s, b) to 3 instead: the flow also stays 7, but now the cut nearest s is \{s, a, b, c, d\} as well, because \{s\} costs 8 and only the cut on the t side is minimal. To increase the flow, every minimum cut must get more capacity.

E5. With c(b, d) = 12 the maximum flow is 20, and the minimum cut is S = \{s\} (10 + 10): the old cut \{s, b\} now costs 10 + 12 = 22. With c(c, t) = 12 instead the flow stays 19: c\!\to\!t is not in the minimum cut, so more capacity there is never used.

Where this leads

The algorithm finds a minimum cut in a graph with thousands or millions of nodes. In the next unit we build a graph from an image, so that the cost of every cut is the energy of a segmentation. One max-flow then gives the best segmentation, exactly.
