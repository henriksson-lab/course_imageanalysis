Exercise: images as arrays & geometric transforms

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • read what a 2\times2 matrix does to an image from its columns and determinant,
  • compute a geometric transform backwards, with nearest-neighbour and bilinear interpolation,
  • build an affine transform (rotation about a point) from homogeneous 3\times3 matrices.

Do the pen-and-paper part first, then the interactive part.

By hand

B1. Sketch what the shear H = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} does to the unit square with corners (0,0), (1,0), (1,1), (0,1). What is \det H, and what does that say about the area?

B2. For each matrix, say whether the result is a mirror image, and give the factor by which areas change: (a) \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0\\ 0 & 0.5\end{bmatrix} (b) \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} (c) \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0\\ 0 & -1\end{bmatrix} (d) \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1\\ 0.5 & 0.5\end{bmatrix}

B3. The image is scaled by A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0\\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix} about the origin. Which input position does the output pixel at \mathbf{x}' = (3, 5) come from?

B4. Bilinear interpolation. Four neighbouring pixels have the values I(0,0) = 10, I(1,0) = 20, I(0,1) = 30, I(1,1) = 60 (written as I(x, y)). Estimate I(0.25, 0.5): first interpolate along x on the rows y = 0 and y = 1, then along y. What does nearest-neighbour give?

B5. Write the 3\times3 homogeneous matrix that rotates by 90° counter-clockwise about the point \mathbf{p} = (2, 1), as a product T_{\mathbf{p}}\,R\,T_{-\mathbf{p}}. Check it: where does \mathbf{p} go? Where does (3, 1) go?

Solutions

B1. H keeps (1, 0) and sends (0, 1) to (2, 1): horizontal lines stay horizontal and every point slides sideways by twice its height. The square becomes a parallelogram with corners (0,0), (1,0), (3,1), (2,1). \det H = 1: the area is unchanged.

B2. (a) \det = 1: twice as wide, half as tall, area unchanged, not mirrored. (b) \det = -1: mirrored left–right, area unchanged. (c) \det = 1: a rotation by 180°, not a mirror image. (d) \det = 0.5 - 0.5 = 0: the columns are parallel, the whole image is squashed onto a line; area 0, not invertible.

B3. A^{-1} = \tfrac12 I, so \mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{x}' = (1.5, 2.5): between pixels, so we must interpolate.

B4. Along x at y = 0: 10 + 0.25\,(20 - 10) = 12.5. At y = 1: 30 + 0.25\,(60 - 30) = 37.5. Along y: 12.5 + 0.5\,(37.5 - 12.5) = 25. Nearest neighbour: (0.25, 0.5) is equally far from (0, 0) and (0, 1), so it depends on the tie-breaking rule: 10 or 30. (Rounding 0.5 up gives (0, 1), value 30.)

B5. T_{\mathbf{p}}RT_{-\mathbf{p}} = \begin{bmatrix}1&0&2\\0&1&1\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1&0&-2\\0&1&-1\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0&-1&3\\1&0&-1\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}. Check: (2, 1) \mapsto (0\cdot2 - 1 + 3,\; 2 + 0 - 1) = (2, 1), so \mathbf{p} stays fixed. (3, 1) \mapsto (-1 + 3,\; 3 - 1) = (2, 2): the point one step to the right of \mathbf{p} ends up one step above it, a quarter turn counter-clockwise.

Explore

The same ideas on a picture: a drawn cell (with an “F” in the corner, so mirror images are easy to spot), centred on the origin. Drag the red and blue handles (the columns of A) or use the presets and the rotation slider.

E1. Drag the blue handle to make the shear H from B1. Does the frame (solid outline) look like your sketch?

E2. Before clicking, predict each preset from its columns: Rotate 30°, Scale, Reflect, Misaligned. Which one gives a mirror image? Then set the rotation slider to 180°: is that a mirror image too?

E3. Click Collapse, then slowly drag the blue handle away from the red one. When exactly does the cell become a line?

E4. Click Scale and look closely at the edges of the cell and the F. The demo uses nearest-neighbour lookup. What would bilinear interpolation change?

Solutions

E1. The red handle stays at (1, 0); put the blue one at (2, 1). Horizontal lines stay horizontal and every point slides sideways by 2 times its height: the x-axis stays put, the top edge of the frame (height 1.5) slides 3 units to the right and the bottom edge 3 units to the left, so the corners leave the view. The lean is the same as in the B1 sketch, where the top of the unit square (height 1) slides 2 units.

E2. Rotate 30°: columns (0.87, 0.5) and (-0.5, 0.87), the picture turns counter-clockwise. Scale: columns (2, 0) and (0, 0.5), twice as wide, half as tall. Reflect: column 1 is (-1, 0), so the picture is mirrored left–right and the F reads backwards. Misaligned: tilted and skewed, as two images of the same sample often are before registration. Rotating by 180° turns the F upside down, but it is not a mirror image: turn your head (or the screen) and it reads normally again. Only the reflection flips it (\det = -1).

E3. Exactly when the two columns lie on one line through the origin, i.e. when \det A = 0. Then every point x\cdot\text{col 1} + y\cdot\text{col 2} is on that line, so the whole picture is squashed onto it. As soon as the columns point in different directions, the picture is a (thin) parallelogram again.

E4. In the horizontal direction each input pixel now covers two output pixels, so edges look blocky and staircase-like. Bilinear interpolation would blend neighbouring values, giving smooth ramps instead of steps, at the price of introducing grey levels that were not in the input (bad for label images, where every value is an object ID).

Where this leads

Finding the matrix that aligns two images (registration) is an optimisation problem over the entries of A and \mathbf{t}; this unit only applies a given transform. Next: changing pixel values using their neighbours.
