# Plan: a day on integral images (summed-area tables)

Internal working doc (plan/ is not rendered). Status: draft for discussion, 2026-11-12.

**Course map (2026-11-12):** elective, group "Detection" (Hough, integral images); entry after core day 2 (edges, blobs and ridges). Owns the integral image and boosting.

## Goal of the day

After the day a student can:

1. compute a **1D prefix sum** and any range sum as a difference of two entries, by hand, and relate it to $\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)$;
2. build the **2D summed-area table** (integral image) of a small image with the one-pass recursion, and compute the sum over **any rectangle with four lookups** (inclusion–exclusion), at a cost that does not depend on the size of the rectangle;
3. compute **box filters of any size** in $O(1)$ per pixel, compare the cost with direct convolution ($O(k^2)$) and separable filtering ($O(k)$), and explain why **repeated box filters approach a Gaussian** (central limit theorem, variances add);
4. compute the **local mean, variance and standard deviation** in any window from the integral images of $I$ and $I^2$, use them for **local contrast normalisation**, and recognise the **numerical traps**: overflow (how many bits the table needs), float32 rounding of large sums, and cancellation in $E[I^2] - E[I]^2$, with their fixes (wider integers, modular differences, subtracting an offset);
5. explain **Haar-like features** (two-, three-, four-rectangle differences in 6, 8 and 9 lookups) and how the **Viola–Jones** detector selects a few of them from a huge pool with boosting and orders them in an **attentional cascade**, and why this made real-time detection possible;
6. build a **multi-scale feature stack** (box means, standard deviations, differences of boxes, Haar responses) as input for a pixel classifier, and say why the integral image makes large scales free.

Why it fits this audience: the whole day is the fundamental theorem of calculus made
discrete. A prefix sum is $F$, a range sum is $F(b) - F(a)$, and the four-corner formula is
the 2D version for a double integral over a rectangle. The local variance is the
$E[X^2] - E[X]^2$ formula from statistics, and the Gaussian limit of repeated boxes is the
central limit theorem. Every step can be done by hand on a 5×5 image.

### 1. `integral-image/`: Precompute once, sum any rectangle in four lookups

- Picture first: "what is the sum of this row of pixels from 3 to 7? and from 2 to 9? and a thousand more such questions?" Adding up each time costs the length of the range. Instead, write down the **running total** once; then every range sum is **one subtraction**: $S[b] - S[a-1]$. Worked on 8 numbers on the board.
- The same picture as calculus: the running total is the antiderivative, the range sum is $F(b) - F(a)$. Bar chart with the cumulative curve over it; the range sum is the rise of the curve.
- 2D: $S(x, y)$ = sum of all pixels above and to the left of (and including) $(x, y)$. A **rectangle sum** from the four corners: $D - B - C + A$; picture with the four overlapping regions shaded (the top-left block is subtracted twice, so it is added back once).
- Building the table in **one pass**: each entry from its left, upper and upper-left neighbours plus the pixel. Equivalent: cumulative sums along rows, then along columns (two 1D passes). Same raster fill as the DP cost table (one-line link to the dynamic-programming elective: sum instead of min).
- **Cost**: building $O(NM)$, about 3 additions per pixel; each rectangle sum 3 additions **whatever its size**. Counting example: a 101×101 window directly costs 10 200 additions, with the table 3. Price: one extra image of wider numbers (pointer to unit 2 for how wide).
- Indexing convention: pad with a zero row and column so that the corner formula needs no special cases at the border; say it on one slide and use it everywhere (demos, exercises).
- *The math: from $\int$ to $\sum$*: $F(x) = \int_0^x f$, $\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)$; in 2D $\Phi(x, y) = \int_0^x\!\int_0^y f$ and $\iint_{[a,b]\times[c,d]} f = \Phi(b,d) - \Phi(a,d) - \Phi(b,c) + \Phi(a,c)$ (inclusion–exclusion of four quadrants). Discrete: $S(i,j) = I(i,j) + S(i-1,j) + S(i,j-1) - S(i-1,j-1)$ (proof by the same picture); the difference operator $\Delta_x\Delta_y S = I$ undoes the summation, as differentiation undoes integration. In $d$ dimensions: $2^d$ corners with signs $(-1)^{d - \#\text{upper corners}}$.
- **Demo `satstep`** (new, main demo of the unit): `data-mode="1d"`: an editable row of 8–16 values (drag bar heights), the running sum drawn over it as a staircase, two draggable markers $a, b$; the range sum is shown both as the shaded bars and as the height difference of the staircase, with a readout "direct: 6 additions / table: 1 subtraction". A *smooth* toggle replaces the bars by a curve and the staircase by $F$ (the calculus picture). `data-mode="2d"`: an editable 5×5 to 8×8 integer image (click ±), the table next to it. *Step* fills the next table cell, highlighting the pixel and the three table entries it uses; *fill all*; *reset*. A draggable, resizable rectangle on the image: its four corner entries in the table light up with their signs (+ − − +), the four-term sum is written out, and a counter compares additions "direct vs table" (also cumulated over all rectangles tried). Presets: "by-hand exercise image" (identical to the exercise), "1×N row is the 1D case", "rectangle touching the border (the zero padding)", "a single pixel from four lookups".
- By hand: (a) prefix sums of 8 numbers and three range sums; (b) the 5×5 table of a given image with values 0–9, two rectangle sums by four lookups, checked by direct addition; (c) how many additions for all 3×3 window sums of a 100×100 image, directly vs with the table. *(math)* (d) prove the corner formula from the recursion; (e) the 3D version: how many corners, which signs.

### 2. `box-filters/`: Box filters of any size, local mean and variance

- Recall (one line, `kernels/`): the box blur is the mean over a $k\times k$ window. Direct cost $k^2$ per pixel; separable (rows then columns) $2k$; a **running sum** along a row (add the pixel entering, subtract the one leaving) $O(1)$ in 1D; the integral image $O(1)$ in 2D, for **every** window size at once, and for windows that vary from pixel to pixel.
- Border rule for free: clip the window to the image and divide by the number of pixels actually inside (no dark frame, unlike "zeros" in `kernels/`).
- **Repeated boxes look Gaussian**: box ∗ box = triangle (tent), three boxes ≈ a smooth bell. Each pass adds the box's variance; the shape tends to a Gaussian by the central limit theorem (sum of independent uniform offsets). Three passes are usually enough; in 2D the square box becomes nearly round after 2–3 passes. Gaussian blur of any $\sigma$ at constant cost per pixel.
- **Local statistics**: two tables, one of $I$ and one of $I^2$, give the local mean $m$ and the local variance $s^2 = \overline{I^2} - m^2$ in any window. Pictures on the micrograph: the mean is a blur, the standard deviation map is bright at edges and in textured regions (a simple **texture** feature).
- **Local contrast normalisation**: $(I - m)/(s + \varepsilon)$ makes a faint and a bright region look alike; the window size sets what counts as "local"; $\varepsilon$ stops the noise in flat background from being amplified. Pointer slide: the same $m$ and $s$ give the Niblack and Sauvola thresholds of day 6; the variance normalisation of every detection window in unit 4.
- **Numbers that get too big** (recall from day 3, one line each, then applied):
  - **Overflow**: the last table entry is the sum of the whole image. Bits needed: bit depth + $\log_2$ of the pixel count. 8-bit 1024×1024: $8 + 20 = 28$ bits, fits a 32-bit integer; the table of $I^2$ needs $16 + 20 = 36$ bits; a 16-bit 2048×2048 image needs 38 bits for $I$ already.
  - A rescue with integers: unsigned **wrap-around** arithmetic still gives the correct rectangle sum as long as the **rectangle's** sum fits, even if the table entries wrapped (the errors cancel in $D - B - C + A$).
  - **Float32** stores integers exactly only up to $2^{24} \approx 1.7\cdot10^7$: the sum of a 256×256 8-bit image is just under that, a 512×512 one is not. Far from the origin the table entries are large, and the rounding error of a small difference grows: the bottom-right of the image gets noisier statistics than the top-left.
  - **Cancellation** in $\overline{I^2} - m^2$: in a bright, flat region both terms are about $200^2$ and the difference about 1; float rounding gives wrong or even **negative** variances. Fixes: subtract an offset (the global mean, or the middle grey level) before building the tables (variance is unchanged by a shift); integer tables; or float64.
- *The math*: box of width $k$ (odd) has variance $(k^2 - 1)/12$; $n$ passes give $n(k^2-1)/12$; matching a Gaussian's $\sigma$ with $n$ boxes (Wells 1986); the Fourier picture: the box's transform is a sinc, its $n$-th power is sinc$^n$, close to a Gaussian near zero and with small side lobes. Variance identity $\operatorname{Var} = E[X^2] - E[X]^2$ and its shift invariance $\operatorname{Var}(X - c) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$; the wrap-around argument as arithmetic modulo $2^{32}$; a rough error bound: rounding error of an entry ≈ $2^{-24}\times$ its size, so the error of a window mean grows with the distance from the origin and shrinks with the window area.
- **Demo `boxspeed`** (new): the micrograph at 512×512 (upsampled crop) or a test pattern. Window-size slider $k = 1$–$101$; methods *direct*, *separable*, *running sum*, *integral image*. Shows the blurred output and a plot of the **operation count per pixel vs $k$** for the four methods (exact, from formulas and counters), plus the **measured time** in ms (median of several runs; labelled as machine-dependent). Tab *repeated boxes*: passes $n = 1$–$4$; the 1D impulse response (bars) against the Gaussian of the same variance (curve), the 2D impulse response as an image with a circle overlay (the square becomes round), and the maximum difference to the Gaussian. Presets: "direct at $k = 51$ is slow", "integral image: flat line", "one box: square artefacts", "three boxes ≈ Gaussian".
- **Demo `localstats`** (new): `cell256.png` (256×256, credit as in the other plans) and a synthetic image (patches of different brightness and contrast on a gradient, plus noise). Panels: input, local mean, local standard deviation, contrast-normalised; window slider; $\varepsilon$ slider; a draggable profile line showing $I$, $m$, $m \pm s$ along it. Tab *precision*: accumulator *float32 / float64 / 32-bit integer (wrapping)*, *subtract offset* toggle, image brightness offset slider (add 0–200 to the image to make cancellation visible), image size 256 / 1024 (tiled); shows the error map of the standard deviation against an exact two-pass computation, the largest error, and a count of negative variances. ### 3. `rect-features/`: Rectangle features: Haar-like features and feature stacks

- Question: what simple numbers describe "what is around this pixel" or "what is in this window", and are cheap enough to compute millions of times?
- **Haar-like features**: the sum over one rectangle minus the sum over an adjacent one. Two-rectangle (edge, horizontal or vertical): 6 lookups, because the shared corners are read once; three-rectangle (line / bar): 8 lookups; four-rectangle (checkerboard / diagonal): 9 lookups; centre–surround (a box minus a larger box): 8. Pictures of the templates on an eye region of a cartoon face and on a cell edge: where the response is large and why.
- Normalisation: divide by the window's standard deviation (from unit 2) so the response does not depend on illumination contrast.
- **The pool is huge**: every type × every position × every width and height in a 24×24 window gives about 160 000 features (count for one type by hand). Each costs the same few lookups regardless of size. Far more features than pixels: overcomplete.
- **Link to the Haar wavelets** (wavelets elective): the 2D Haar subbands are two- and four-rectangle features at dyadic sizes and positions, forming an orthonormal basis. Haar-like features drop orthogonality and take all sizes and positions; they are for **detection**, not reconstruction. One comparison slide.
- **Feature stacks for pixel classification** (one recall slide; the stack, the painting loop and the classifier are owned by day 14): the **box group** added here: local box mean, standard deviation, difference of two box means (centre–surround, ≈ difference of Gaussians from day 2, a blob detector), Haar edge responses in $x$ and $y$, at scales 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 px. With the integral image every scale costs the same: large context is free. Gaussian-based stacks are the usual choice; box stacks are their fast approximation (unit 2). The group plugs into the shared `pixelclass` shell as `data-stack="box"`.
- One-line pointer: the region-level counterparts (intensity and texture features per segmented object) are on day 11.
- *The math*: a feature as an inner product $f = \langle w, I\rangle$ with a piecewise-constant $w \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$ (or weighted so that $\sum w = 0$: zero response on flat regions); the lookup count as the number of distinct corners; the pool size $\sum_{w,h} (W - aw + 1)(H - bh + 1)$ for a template of $a\times b$ cells; the response of an edge feature to a linear ramp (proportional to the gradient: a box-smoothed derivative, like Sobel from `kernels/`).
- **Demo `haarexplore`** (new): an image (`cell256.png`, a synthetic cartoon face scene, a synthetic scene of bright cells), a feature-template palette (2-rect horizontal/vertical, 3-rect, 4-rect, centre–surround). Drag and resize the template on the image: live response value, the corners read (dots, numbered) and "lookups: 6 vs pixels summed: 1 200". A *response map* toggle computes the template at every position (heat map, diverging colours). Tab *stack* (`data-mode="stack"`): the feature stack as a grid of thumbnails (rows = feature type, columns = scale); click a pixel to see its feature vector as bars; paint a few pixels as class A and B and see a scatter plot of any two chosen features, coloured by class, to judge which features separate (no classifier here: hand-off to `pixelclass` of day 14; a single threshold line on one feature is allowed, as the bridge to unit 4). Presets: "eyes are darker than cheeks", "vertical edge feature on a cell boundary", "centre–surround finds nuclei of the right size", "which feature separates nucleus from cytoplasm?".
- By hand: (a) a two-rectangle feature on a 6×6 image with given table: list the 6 lookups and their signs, compute the response; (b) the same for a three-rectangle feature (8 lookups); (c) count the horizontal two-rectangle features in an 8×8 window; (d) which of four given templates responds most to a pictured vertical bar, and with which sign. *(math)* (e) show that the 4-rectangle feature needs 9 distinct corners and the 2-rectangle 6; (f) the response of a horizontal edge feature of size $2w\times h$ to $I(x, y) = ax + b$.

### 4. `viola-jones/`: Boosted detectors and the attentional cascade

- The detection task: slide a 24×24 window over every position and scale of an image (hundreds of thousands of windows), decide face / not face for each. Almost all windows are background.
- Scales without an image pyramid: scale the **features** (rectangles grow, cost unchanged), not the image. Variance normalisation per window from the $I^2$ table (unit 2).
- Recall (one slide, day 14): a classifier, a training set and a test set, the error rate.
- **Weak classifier** (decision stump, defined here in one slide): one feature, one threshold, one direction; a depth-1 tree (day 14 grows full trees and forests from the same split search). Alone each is barely better than guessing.
- **Boosting**, concept level: start with equal weights on all training examples; each round pick the stump with the smallest **weighted** error; increase the weight of the examples it got wrong, so the next stump focuses on them; the final classifier is a weighted vote. Feature **selection** falls out: of 160 000 features, a few hundred are ever chosen. The first two chosen features for faces: "eye region darker than the cheeks" and "eyes darker than the bridge of the nose" (pictures).
- **Attentional cascade**: a sequence of stages, each a small boosted classifier tuned to keep (almost) all faces and reject a fair share of background. A window is rejected at the first stage that says no; only promising windows reach the expensive later stages. On average only about 10 features are evaluated per window. Detection rate and false-positive rate of the cascade are **products** of the stage rates.
- Why it was a breakthrough (2001): real-time face detection on a 2001 desktop CPU, from three ideas that each give a factor: integral image (cheap features), boosting (few features), cascade (few windows get many features). Overlapping detections are merged; failure modes: rotation and profile faces (rotated features, unit 5; separate detectors), occlusion, the training set's biases (who is in the data decides who is detected well; one slide). Matching detections to ground truth and AP: pointer to day 16.
- Beyond faces: the same detector for cells, nuclei, mitoses; modern detectors use learned convolutional features (pointer to days 15 and 18), but the cascade idea and integral-image features live on where compute is tiny.
- *The math*: AdaBoost (Freund & Schapire): weighted error $\varepsilon_t$, vote weight $\alpha_t = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}$, weight update $w_i \leftarrow w_i\, e^{-\alpha_t y_i h_t(x_i)}$ and renormalise; training error $\le \prod_t 2\sqrt{\varepsilon_t(1-\varepsilon_t)}$, which shrinks as long as every $\varepsilon_t < 1/2$; the best stump threshold for one feature in one pass over the sorted values (cumulative weighted sums: a prefix sum again). Cascade: $D = \prod_i d_i$, $F = \prod_i f_i$, expected features per window $= \sum_i n_i \prod_{j<i} p_j$ with $p_j$ the pass rate of stage $j$.
- **Demo `boostdetect`** (new, main demo of the unit): `data-target="faces|cells"`. A seeded training set of 24×24 patches: positives from a synthetic generator (cartoon faces with random brightness, contrast, small shifts and scale, glasses/no glasses; or bright cells with random size and texture), negatives cropped from synthetic scenes without targets. A feature pool subsampled on a stride-2 grid (several thousand features; size shown). Panel *boost*: button *add one stump* (or *add 10*), the chosen feature drawn on the mean positive patch, the training examples as a strip whose dot size is their current weight (mistakes grow), and training and test error vs rounds. Panel *cascade*: build 3–6 stages with a per-stage target detection rate slider; bar chart of windows surviving each stage; readout "average features per window". Panel *detect*: a 256×256 synthetic test scene, sliding window at 3–4 scales, detections drawn, rejected windows coloured by the stage that rejected them; toggle *no cascade* to compare features evaluated. Presets: "first two stumps", "boosting focuses on the hard examples", "a single strong classifier vs a cascade: same accuracy, 20× fewer features", "a rotated face is missed", "training set without glasses". Training uses precomputed sorted feature values; if a round takes over ~30 ms per click in TypeScript, precompute the first 200 rounds offline and ship them (see open questions).
- By hand: (a) one boosting round on 6 labelled 1D values: best stump, weighted error, new weights; (b) a 3-stage cascade with given $d_i, f_i$: overall detection and false-positive rate, and the expected number of features per window; (c) 10 stages with $d = 0.99$, $f = 0.3$: is $D \ge 0.9$? how small is $F$? *(math)* (d) derive $\alpha_t$ by minimising the exponential loss for one round; (e) show the training-error bound for the case of two rounds.

### 5. (optional / further reading) `integral-more/`: Variations on pre-integration

- **Rotated (45°) summed-area tables** (Lienhart & Maydt 2002): sums over diamond-shaped regions, for tilted Haar-like features; built in two passes.
- **Integral histograms** (Porikli 2005): one table per histogram bin, so the histogram of any rectangle costs $2\times$ number of bins lookups: local medians, local entropy, histogram matching for tracking, orientation histograms. Memory: bins × image.
- **3D integral volumes**: eight corners; box statistics in volumetric microscopy and video (space–time boxes).
- Any per-pixel quantity can be pre-integrated: $I$, $I^2$, $x^p y^q I$ (moment templates, pointer to the moments elective), products of two channels (local correlation), gradient bins.
- **Graphics**: Crow (1984) introduced summed-area tables for texture filtering (average texture over a pixel's footprint); compared with mipmaps (`gpuformats/`, 3D graphics elective): exact rectangles vs dyadic squares, wider numbers vs +1/3 memory.
- **Parallel prefix sums (scan) on GPUs**: a prefix sum looks sequential but runs in $\log_2 n$ parallel steps (Hillis–Steele; work-efficient Blelloch). Picture of the 16-element scan as a ladder diagram. The 2D table = scan along rows, then along columns.
- **Demo**: `satstep` with `data-mode="scan"` (16 elements, step through the $\log_2 16 = 4$ parallel rounds, counting additions vs the sequential scan), and `data-mode="histogram"` (a small `localhist` panel on `cell256.png`: drag a window, its histogram updates from 2 × bins lookups, and a local-median map).
