## Goal of the day

After the day a student can:

1. explain why a semantic mask from a U-Net is **not** a set of objects, and turn a network's output into **instances** by four routes: a boundary class + labelling, a predicted **distance map** + watershed, **star-convex polygons** + suppression, and a predicted **flow field** followed to its sinks; predict which route fails on touching, elongated and non-convex cells; say what **panoptic** segmentation adds;
2. compute the **IoU** of two boxes, run greedy **non-maximum suppression** by hand, and explain how a detector proposes boxes (**anchors** vs **centre heatmaps**), and why NMS can delete a true neighbour in a crowd;
3. compute a small **self-attention** step by hand (scores, softmax, weighted average), explain it as a **learned, content-dependent weighted average** next to a convolution's **fixed, position-only** weights, and say what a transformer gains (global context in one layer) and pays (no built-in locality, cost growing with the square of the number of patches, more data);
4. explain **transfer learning**, the difference between **frozen features** + a small classifier and **fine-tuning**, and the ideas of **self-supervised pretraining** (contrastive, masked autoencoding) and of a **promptable** segmenter; judge from a reliability diagram whether a network's probabilities are **calibrated**, and say when a **classical method** is the better choice;
5. describe a **diffusion model**: forward noising with a **noise schedule**, a network trained to **denoise**, sampling as **repeated denoising** from pure noise, and **conditioning** on a label or a measurement;
6. explain why a **generative restoration** can produce sharp, plausible and **wrong** detail (hallucination), tell a posterior mean from a posterior sample, and decide what may still be **measured** on a restored image;
7. *(math)* write attention as $\mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt d)V$ and show it is permutation-equivariant; write the forward process $\mathbf x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ and the link between the best denoiser and the score (Tweedie); derive the exact posterior of a linear Gaussian measurement under a Gaussian-mixture prior; show that a posterior sample has twice the expected squared error of the posterior mean.

Why it fits this audience: every idea of the day reduces to things they already have. An
instance map is a landscape for the watershed day's flooding; IoU is set arithmetic;
attention is a softmax-weighted average of vectors (a matrix product); a contrastive loss is
a softmax over dot products; diffusion is "add Gaussian noise, learn to remove it", and the
toy cases are exact with the Gaussian mixtures of the GMM day. The day teaches the **ideas
behind** current architectures; no architecture or product is taught as a tool.

## Units (one folder each: lec, quiz, exercise with #explore)

### 1. `instances/`: From masks to objects: instance segmentation and detection outputs

*Guiding question: the network says "nucleus" for every nucleus pixel. Which pixels belong to which nucleus?*

- Recall (one slide): `unetseg` gives a probability per pixel; touching nuclei come out as one blob (the "touching nuclei merge" preset of day 36). Semantic = a class per pixel; **instance** = an object identity per pixel; **panoptic** = both at once (every pixel gets a class, and countable "things" also get an identity, while "stuff" like background or tissue does not).
- **Why post-processing is unavoidable**: a convolutional network is translation-equivariant and has no fixed number of outputs, while the number of objects varies from 0 to 1000. So the network predicts a **map that makes separation easy**, and a classical step (labelling, watershed, flooding, suppression) produces the objects. Four such maps, each a picture first:
  1. **Boundary class** (background / interior / boundary): label the connected interiors, then grow them back. Fails when one boundary pixel is missed: a one-pixel bridge merges two cells.
  2. **Distance map**: the network regresses, for each pixel, its distance to the object's border (normalised). Peaks = centres; then exactly the watershed day's `ws-distance/` pipeline (h-maxima markers, flood $-D$ inside the mask). Advantage over the distance transform of a mask: the network can put a deep neck where the mask has none (it has seen the image, not just the mask). Fails on elongated cells (ridge with several peaks: oversplitting).
  3. **Star-convex polygons**: for each pixel, the network predicts an "objectness" score and the distances to the border along $n$ fixed rays (e.g. 32). Each pixel thus proposes a polygon; overlapping proposals are pruned by **non-maximum suppression** (unit 2 teaches it; previewed here). Works well for round/oval nuclei; fails by construction on shapes that are not star-convex (a C-shaped or kidney-shaped cell: no centre sees the whole border).
  4. **Flow fields**: the network predicts, for each pixel, a 2D vector pointing along a path *inside* the cell towards its centre (training targets come from heat diffusion from the centre, so the paths bend around a non-convex shape). Follow the arrows from every pixel; pixels that end in the same **sink** form one object. Handles long and bent cells; fails when the predicted field is noisy (spurious sinks: oversplitting) or when two cells share a smooth field (merging).
- Generalist cell segmenters trained on many datasets use routes 2–4 (one line, no names on slides).
- Choosing: round nuclei → polygons or distance; irregular cytoplasm, bacteria, neurites → flows; anything → look at the predicted map, not only the final labels. Errors in the map translate to typical object errors (merge, split, missed dim cell), which day 37's metrics count.
- **Detection as another output** (bridge to unit 2): instead of a map, predict a list of objects (box + score, optionally a mask inside each box). Instance segmentation by "detect, then segment inside the box" is the other big family (one slide, picture only).
- **Panoptic** briefly: a semantic head and an instance head, merged by a rule (instance labels win on "thing" pixels). One slide with a tissue example (nuclei = things, stroma/background = stuff).
- The math: the four regression targets written out: boundary as a class; $D(p) = \min_{q \notin O}\|p-q\|/\max_{O}D$; star-convex rays $r_k(p) = \max\{r : p + s\,\mathbf u_k \in O\ \forall s \le r\}$, $\mathbf u_k = (\cos\tfrac{2\pi k}{n}, \sin\tfrac{2\pi k}{n})$, and the definition of star-convexity; flow target $\mathbf v = \nabla h/\|\nabla h\|$ for $h$ the steady heat distribution with a source at the centre, flow following as Euler steps $\mathbf x_{j+1} = \mathbf x_j + \eta\,\mathbf v(\mathbf x_j)$ whose fixed points are the sinks; losses (cross-entropy for classes, squared/absolute error for distances and flows). Panoptic quality $\mathrm{PQ} = \mathrm{SQ}\times\mathrm{RQ}$ (mean IoU of matched segments × the F1 of day 37's matching at τ = 0.5), unless it moves to day 37.
- **Demo `instancemaps`** (new, main demo of the unit): a seeded synthetic fluorescence image, 128×128, of ~15 cells with ground-truth labels (shared nuclei generator, extended with elongated, bent/C-shaped and tightly touching cells). The "network output" is **simulated**: the exact target map computed from the ground truth, then degraded by **error sliders** the student controls: *noise* (a smooth seeded random field added to the map), *blur* (smooths necks away), *contact bias* (weakens the boundary/neck exactly where two cells touch, the typical real failure), *missed dim cells* (fades the map on the dimmest cells). Representation menu: boundary class / distance / star-convex ($n$ rays slider 4–64) / flows. Panels: input; predicted map (class colours, distance as colour + iso-lines, a polygon per clicked pixel, or arrows on a coarse grid with a few particle trajectories); result labels; error overlay with merges and splits marked, and F1 and matching accuracy at τ = 0.5 from day 37's `lib/eval.ts`. Post-processing controls matching the route: threshold, $h$, NMS IoU threshold, number of flow steps. *Animate* for flows (particles move to the sinks) and for watershed (the flood level rises). Presets: "semantic mask merges the touching pair", "boundary class: one gap merges", "distance + watershed separates the pair", "elongated cell: distance oversplits", "C-shaped cell: polygons cannot fit it", "flows follow the bend", "noisy flows: too many sinks", "contact bias: every route merges", "crowded tissue". Labelled on screen as *simulated network output* so nobody mistakes it for a trained model.
- Optional weight set (see Open questions): a real tiny U-Net with the same four heads, trained offline on the same generator (`train_instnet.py`), so students can compare simulated errors with real ones.
- By hand: (a) a 6×8 three-class map with one missing boundary pixel: label the interiors (4-connectivity) and count objects; (b) a 1D distance profile across two touching cells with peaks 6 and 5 and neck 4: how many objects for $h = 1$, $h = 2.5$? (recalls the watershed day); (c) star-convex: for a given small mask, draw 8 rays from two candidate centres and decide whether the mask is star-convex from either; sketch a shape that is not star-convex from any point; (d) flows: a 1D flow field on 10 pixels ($\pm1$ arrows, with one wrong arrow): follow from every pixel, list the sinks, find the split it causes; (e) for each of four described cell types, choose a representation and say what will go wrong. *(math)*: show that every convex set is star-convex and give a star-convex set that is not convex; show that following $\nabla h/\|\nabla h\|$ from any interior point ends at the source if $h$ has a single maximum in the cell (sketch: $h$ increases along the path).

### 2. `detection/`: Finding objects as boxes: IoU, anchors, heatmaps and NMS

*Guiding question: how does a network output "there are 12 cells, here", when the number is not known in advance?*

- **Boxes** as the simplest object description: centre, width, height (4 numbers) + class + score. Enough for counting, tracking and cropping; poor for elongated diagonal objects (a box around a diagonal rod is mostly background).
- **IoU**: recall from day 37 (overlap / union of two objects); for boxes it has a closed form from the corner coordinates. Worked example with numbers. Recall (one slide, no re-teaching): day 37 matches predictions to truth at an IoU threshold, counts F1 and matching accuracy, and ranks scored detections into a precision–recall curve whose area is the **average precision (AP)**; for detectors, AP is often averaged over several IoU thresholds (one line). This unit adds what happens *before* evaluation: where the scored boxes come from and how duplicates are removed.
- **How candidates are produced**: the network looks at every position of a coarse grid (the feature map of day 36) and makes a guess there.
  - **Anchors**: at every grid cell, a few template boxes of preset sizes and shapes; the network predicts for each "is there an object?" and a small correction of the box. An object is learned only by anchors that overlap it well, so the preset sizes must fit the objects (tiny or very elongated objects match none).
  - **Anchor-free / centre heatmaps**: the network outputs a **heatmap** of object centres (a small bump at each centre) plus, at each pixel, the box size. Objects = local maxima of the heatmap: the same peak picking as the Hough accumulator. No preset shapes.
- Either way, **each object gets many overlapping candidates**. **Non-maximum suppression** (greedy): sort by score, keep the best, delete every remaining candidate with IoU > $t$ with it, repeat. By hand on five boxes.
- **What NMS gets wrong**: in a crowd of touching cells the true neighbour overlaps more than $t$ and is deleted (too low $t$); with $t$ too high duplicates survive. One threshold cannot fix both: the reason detection is hard in dense tissue, and one reason why the map-based routes of unit 1 are preferred for crowded cells. Star-convex polygons are NMS with polygon IoU (link back to unit 1).
- Three meanings of "non-maximum suppression" in the course (Canny, Hough, here): one slide lining them up.
- Training in one line each: the class/score with a cross-entropy (with most locations being background: a loss that down-weights easy background examples), the box with a regression loss; matching candidates to truth by IoU (anchors) or by the centre pixel (heatmaps).
- The math: $\mathrm{IoU}(A,B) = |A\cap B|/|A\cup B|$ for axis-aligned boxes in closed form ($\max/\min$ of coordinates); greedy NMS in pseudocode, $O(n^2)$; the box parameterisation relative to an anchor ($t_x = (x - x_a)/w_a$, $t_w = \log(w/w_a)$) and why the log; Gaussian centre targets $Y(p) = \exp(-\|p - c\|^2/2\sigma^2)$ and peak picking as a 3×3 max filter; the focal-loss factor $(1-p)^\gamma$ in one line.
- **Demo `nmsboxes`** (new; analytic, no trained weights): a synthetic cell image (same generator) with candidate boxes drawn on it. Mode *IoU*: two draggable/resizable boxes, the intersection shaded, the areas and IoU printed live. Mode *NMS*: a seeded set of candidates (a simulated detector: several jittered boxes per true cell with scores, plus a few false positives) with editable scores (click, ±); sliders IoU threshold $t$ and score threshold; *step* runs one round of greedy NMS (the kept box outlined, the suppressed ones greyed with their IoU printed), *run*, *reset*; ground truth toggle and readouts from `lib/eval.ts`: TP/FP/FN, F1 and matching accuracy at τ = 0.5, and the AP of the surviving scored boxes (so students see NMS's threshold move AP). Mode *anchors*: a grid of anchors (stride and 1–3 shapes as controls); each true object shows its best anchor IoU, anchors above 0.5 highlighted, unmatched objects marked red. Mode *heatmap*: a simulated centre heatmap (bump width and noise sliders), its local maxima (window size slider) and the boxes they give. Presets: "IoU of two boxes", "duplicates removed", "touching cells: NMS deletes the neighbour", "threshold too high: duplicates survive", "diagonal rod: a box is a poor shape", "small cell: no anchor matches", "heatmap peaks = objects", "noisy heatmap: two peaks in one cell".
- By hand: (a) IoU of $[0,4]\times[0,4]$ and $[2,6]\times[2,6]$ ($4/28 \approx 0.14$), and of two boxes offset by 1 pixel; (b) greedy NMS on five given boxes with scores, for $t = 0.3$ and $t = 0.7$; which true cell is lost; (c) a 10×10 anchor and a 4×4 cell with the same centre: IoU $= 0.16$, matched or not at 0.5? Which anchor size would match?; (d) find the local maxima of a 5×5 heatmap with a 3×3 window. *(math)*: derive the closed form of box IoU; show that IoU is scale-invariant and that $1 - \mathrm{IoU}$ gives no gradient when boxes do not overlap; why $\log(w/w_a)$ and not $w - w_a$.

### 3. `attention/`: Attention, transformers and pretrained models

*Guiding question: a 3×3 kernel only sees its neighbours. How can a network relate a pixel to the whole image, and where do networks come from that nobody trained on my images?*

Concept part A: attention (about 45 min).

- Recall (one slide): receptive field of a conv stack grows by $k-1$ per layer (`receptive` demo); seeing a 200-pixel structure needs many layers or pooling. The kernel weights are the **same** at every position and do not depend on the content.
- **Patches as tokens**: cut the image into 16×16 patches, flatten each into a vector, map it linearly to an embedding, add a **position code** (otherwise the network cannot tell where a patch came from). The image is now a set of $N$ vectors.
- **Self-attention as a learned weighted average**: each patch asks a question (query), each patch offers a label (key) and a content (value). The weight of patch $j$ for patch $i$ = how well $i$'s query matches $j$'s key (dot product), turned into weights that sum to 1 (softmax). Output for $i$ = weighted average of all values. By hand with three tokens.
- **Convolution vs attention**, one picture each: convolution = fixed weights, by **relative position**, local; attention = weights computed **from the content**, any distance, every layer global. Attention with weights depending only on relative position *is* a convolution (one slide). Attention on patch similarity with fixed (not learned) queries and keys is the **non-local means** denoiser (links to day 4).
- A **transformer block** = attention + a small per-token network, with residual connections (recall day 36), stacked. Several attention "heads" = several weightings in parallel.
- **Costs and consequences**: $N$ patches give $N^2$ weights per layer (64×64 patches of a 1024² image: 16 million); no built-in locality or translation equivariance, so it must be **learned from data**: transformers need much more data or pretraining (part B). Hybrids (conv early, attention late) are common.

Concept part B: pretrained and foundation models (about 45 min).

- **Transfer learning**: early layers of any image network learn edges, blobs, textures (day 36); reuse a network trained on a huge dataset. Two ways: **frozen features** (use the network's internal vectors, *embeddings*, as features and train only a small classifier on top: few labels needed, fast, safe) vs **fine-tuning** (continue training all or the last layers on your data: better with enough labels, can overfit and forget with few). Picture: accuracy vs number of labelled images for "from scratch", "frozen", "fine-tuned" (precomputed curve).
- **Self-supervised pretraining** (no labels):
  - **contrastive**: two augmented views of the same crop (rotated, blurred, recoloured) should have similar embeddings, views of different crops dissimilar; the network learns what stays the same under the augmentations, so the choice of augmentations decides what it ignores (ignore colour → cannot tell stains apart);
  - **masked autoencoding**: hide 75 % of the patches, predict them from the rest (a natural pretext for patch tokens; the network must learn what cells look like to fill them in).
- **Foundation models**: very large networks pretrained this way on millions of images, used through frozen features or light fine-tuning. **Promptable segmentation**: the input is the image plus a **prompt** (a click, a box), the output a mask for "the thing at the click". An idea, shown with a toy (demo), and with its limits: trained mostly on photographs, it may not know what a cell boundary is in a phase-contrast image; a click gives *a* mask, not a consistent definition of an object across 1000 images.
- **Practical judgement** (the closing slides of the day's learning half):
  - **Data requirements**: rough orders of magnitude (tens of annotated images for a U-Net on one microscope with augmentation; a handful of clicks for a frozen-feature classifier; many thousands for training a transformer from scratch). Annotation quality and consistency matter more than quantity. Augmentation: recall, and the rule "augment with what varies in your experiments, never with what you want to measure" (do not augment intensity if intensity is the readout).
  - **Domain shift**: recall the `unetseg` presets; pretrained features shift too (new microscope, stain, magnification). Test on data from a new session/site (day 37's split rules).
  - **Uncertainty and calibration**: a network says 0.95; is it right 95 % of the time? Reliability diagram (bin by confidence, plot accuracy). Modern networks are often **overconfident**, and more so under domain shift; a single temperature fitted on validation data fixes the in-domain part, not the shift. Disagreement between several networks (an ensemble) or between augmented copies flags doubtful regions.
  - **When classical is better**: few images, strong prior knowledge of the shape (Hough, model fitting), a measurement whose bias must be understood (thresholding a known calibration standard), no annotations and no time, results that must be explainable to a reviewer, or a classical method that already works (check against the Otsu/RF baselines first). Often the best pipeline is a network map + a classical step (unit 1).
- The math: $\mathrm{Attn}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d})V$ with $Q = XW_Q$, $K = XW_K$, $V = XW_V$; why $\sqrt d$ (the dot product of two random unit-variance vectors has variance $d$); permutation equivariance $\mathrm{Attn}(PX) = P\,\mathrm{Attn}(X)$ and why position codes are needed; cost $O(N^2 d)$ vs $O(N k^2 d)$ for convolution; attention with scores depending only on $j - i$ as a convolution; non-local means as attention with $q_i = k_i$ = patch, temperature $h^2$; contrastive (InfoNCE) loss $-\log\frac{\exp(\mathbf z_i^\top\mathbf z_i'/\tau)}{\sum_j\exp(\mathbf z_i^\top\mathbf z_j'/\tau)}$ as a softmax classification of "which is my partner"; masked-patch loss; linear probe = logistic regression on frozen embeddings; expected calibration error $\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_b \frac{n_b}{n}|\mathrm{acc}_b - \mathrm{conf}_b|$ and temperature scaling $\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf z/T)$ (does not change the predicted class).
- **Demo `selfattn`** (new; analytic, hand-set weights, no training): a 64×64 image (synthetic: textured regions, a few cells, a long filament; plus `cell256.png` downsampled) cut into an 8×8 grid of patch tokens. Each token's embedding is shown as a small bar chart of a few hand-made features (mean, contrast, edge orientation) plus a 2D position code. Queries and keys are linear maps of the embedding controlled by two sliders, **content weight** and **position weight** (and an *advanced* panel with the 2×2/4×4 matrices editable), and a temperature. Click a query patch: the 64 attention weights appear as a heat map over the image; next to it, the **fixed 3×3 conv footprint** on the same patch grid (same everywhere, blind to content). Output panel: the attention-weighted average of the values (patch pixels) for every patch, i.e. the image after one attention layer. Toggles: *no position code* + *shuffle patches* (the output shuffles along: permutation equivariance); *receptive field after L layers* strip comparing conv ($L$ slider) with one attention layer. Presets: "position only: attention acts like a blur kernel", "content only: a patch attends to all similar patches, however far" (on the filament and the cells), "non-local means: denoise by averaging similar patches", "temperature to zero: attend to one patch", "no position code: shuffling the image changes nothing but the order", "conv needs 30 layers to see this far".
- **Demo `embedprobe`** (new, **precomputed weights**): the RF day's paint–train–look loop on **frozen learned embeddings**. Images: `cell256.png`, a synthetic multi-texture/cell image with ground truth, and a "second microscope" version of each (shift sliders below). An embedding per pixel (stride 4, upsampled for display) from one of: *raw pixels* (a 9×9 patch, as a baseline), *filter stack* (the RF day's features), *self-supervised* (a tiny CNN encoder, ~20k weights, 16-D output, run live via `lib/cnn.ts`; trained offline, see Shared engineering), optionally *masked-autoencoder* encoder (second weight set). Interactions: (1) *click a pixel*: cosine-similarity map to that pixel's embedding ("what does the network think is like this?"); (2) *paint two classes* → a logistic-regression **linear probe** trained live (a few ms) → probability map and Dice against truth on the synthetic image; (3) *prompt mode*: a click → similarity map thresholded → the connected component containing the click: the **idea** of promptable segmentation in miniature, labelled as a toy; (4) *shift* sliders (brightness, blur, noise, scale): embeddings recomputed live, the probe trained on the original degrades. Presets: "raw pixels: similarity = brightness", "self-supervised: all nuclei light up, whatever their brightness", "five strokes are enough with good features", "filter stack vs learned features", "a click → a mask", "new microscope: the probe breaks".
- **Demo `calibration`** (new, small, analytic): a simulated classifier: scores for two classes drawn from seeded distributions; panels: the histogram of predicted probabilities and the **reliability diagram** with ECE. Sliders: *overconfidence* (sharpens the scores), *temperature* $T$ (the fix), *domain shift* (moves the test distribution after $T$ was fitted on validation). Presets: "calibrated", "overconfident network", "temperature fixes it in-domain", "shift: overconfident again", "accuracy unchanged, calibration changed".
- By hand: (a) attention with three tokens: query $(1,0)$, keys $(1,0), (0,1), (1,1)$, values $10, 0, 4$: scores $1, 0, 1$, weights $0.42, 0.16, 0.42$, output $\approx 5.9$; the same with temperature $\tfrac12$; (b) number of tokens and attention weights for a 512×512 image with 16×16 and 8×8 patches; (c) receptive field of a conv stack vs one attention layer; (d) choose frozen features or fine-tuning for three described projects (20 labelled images; 5000 labelled images from the same microscope; a new imaging modality); (e) which augmentations must a contrastive model *not* use if you later want to classify by stain colour; (f) a 10-row table of confidence and correctness: draw the reliability diagram with two bins and compute ECE; (g) for four described tasks, classical or deep, and why. *(math)*: show permutation equivariance of self-attention without position codes; show that the variance of $\mathbf q^\top\mathbf k$ is $d$ for independent unit-variance entries; show that temperature scaling does not change the argmax; write the InfoNCE loss for a batch of 3 pairs and identify it as a cross-entropy.

### 4. `diffusion/`: Diffusion models and the risk of generated detail

*Guiding question: if a network can remove noise, can it make images from nothing? And if it restores my image, which details are real?*

- **A generative model** (first time in the course, as the VAE day comes after): a recipe to draw *new* images that look like the training images, i.e. samples from the distribution of the data. In 2D: points from a cloud; in images: a cloud in $\mathbb R^{784}$ (the same "images as points" picture the VAE day uses).
- **Forward process**: take a training image, add a little Gaussian noise, again and again, while shrinking the signal; after many steps nothing is left but noise. The **noise schedule** says how fast (how much signal remains at each step); the picture: the 2D cloud blurring into one round Gaussian.
- **The core operation is denoising**: train a network that takes a noisy image and the noise level and predicts the noise (equivalently the clean image). This is ordinary supervised learning with free labels (we added the noise ourselves). The best possible denoiser at high noise does not output a clean image: it outputs the **average of all clean images compatible** with the noisy one (by-hand 1D example: data at $\pm1$, the best guess at high noise is between them).
- **Sampling = repeated denoising**: start from pure noise; at each step, denoise a little and add back a bit less noise; after many steps a sample emerges. The arrows (the direction the denoiser pushes) form a field that points towards the data at every noise level. Few steps: blurry, off-the-cloud samples; many steps: sharp ones.
- **Memorisation vs generalisation**: the *perfect* denoiser of a finite training set only regenerates training images (exact toy in the demo); real networks generalise because they are imperfect in a useful way. One slide; good to know when someone claims a generated image is "new".
- **Conditioning**: give the denoiser a label (class "dividing cell"), a text, or a **measurement** (a blurred image). Pushing harder towards the condition (**guidance**) gives more typical, less diverse samples.
- **Restoration with a generative prior**: a degraded measurement (blurred, noisy, missing pixels) is compatible with many clean images. A classical method (Wiener/Tikhonov, day 4) assumes "smooth" and returns a blurry but cautious answer. A generative prior picks images that look like **training** images: sharp, plausible, and possibly **invented**. Two different outputs:
  - the **posterior mean** (average of all plausible restorations): blurry where uncertain, lowest error on average;
  - a **posterior sample** (one plausible restoration): sharp everywhere, but where the data are weak the detail comes from the prior, not from the sample. Two samples disagree exactly there.
- **Hallucination in scientific imaging**: a restoration can add a nucleolus, a protrusion, a membrane, a dividing cell that was not there, or remove a real one that the prior did not expect (an unusual phenotype is "corrected" towards normal: the most dangerous case in biology and medicine). Higher PSNR (recall day 3) does not mean the details are real.
- **What may be measured afterwards** (rules for the integrity unit): (1) measure on the raw data or on a restoration whose effect on *that* measurement was validated with ground truth; (2) never count or measure structures smaller than the measurement can resolve on a generatively restored image; (3) look at disagreement between several samples or several models and do not trust details where they disagree; (4) never train the prior on the condition you want to discover; (5) report the restoration method and show the raw data next to it. Restoration for **display** and **segmentation help** is different from restoration as **data**.
- The math: forward process $\mathbf x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon$, schedule $\bar\alpha_t$ (linear vs cosine), signal-to-noise ratio $\bar\alpha_t/(1-\bar\alpha_t)$; training loss $\mathbb E\|\boldsymbol\varepsilon - \boldsymbol\varepsilon_\theta(\mathbf x_t, t)\|^2$; **Tweedie**: $\mathbb E[\mathbf x_0\mid\mathbf x_t] = (\mathbf x_t + (1-\bar\alpha_t)\nabla\log p_t(\mathbf x_t))/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$, so a denoiser is a **score** estimator; the exact score of a noised Gaussian mixture (components $\mathcal N(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\boldsymbol\mu_k, \bar\alpha_t\Sigma_k + (1-\bar\alpha_t)I)$, responsibilities as weights); one stochastic and one deterministic sampling step; conditioning by Bayes, $\nabla\log p(\mathbf x\mid y) = \nabla\log p(\mathbf x) + \nabla\log p(y\mid\mathbf x)$, guidance weight $w$; exact posterior of $\mathbf y = A\mathbf x + \mathbf n$ under a mixture prior (a mixture of Gaussians again, weights from the evidence $\mathcal N(\mathbf y; A\boldsymbol\mu_k, A\Sigma_kA^\top + \sigma_n^2I)$); the **perception–distortion** fact: for a Gaussian posterior, a sample has twice the expected squared error of the mean.
- **Demo `diffusion2d`** (new; exact analytic score + one **precomputed** learned score): 2D point clouds as data (presets: two blobs, eight blobs on a ring, a rare small blob next to a big one, two moons approximated by a mixture of many small Gaussians). *Forward* tab: 500 seeded points, a time slider shows $\mathbf x_t$ melting into $\mathcal N(0, I)$; a small plot of the schedule ($\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$, noise level, linear/cosine menu). *Reverse* tab: start from noise, *step* / *run* / *reset*; the score field at the current $t$ drawn as arrows; one highlighted trajectory; controls: number of steps (5–1000), stochastic vs deterministic sampler, score source **exact** (analytic for the mixture) / **learned** (a tiny MLP, see Shared engineering; two snapshots: under-trained, trained) / **exact with tiny component width** (the "memorising" score of a finite training set: every sample lands on a training point). *Condition* menu: none / a chosen blob (exact conditional score) and guidance weight $w$ (0–5). Presets: "noise in, data out", "too few steps: samples between the blobs", "learned score: the rare blob is dropped", "perfect score of 20 points: it only copies", "conditioning on one blob", "strong guidance: all samples in the centre of the blob".
- **Demo `genrestore`** (new; exact, no trained weights): **hallucination** made visible. Images: 32×32 synthetic cells with nucleus and 0–2 nucleoli, a protrusion or a division (generated in TS, seeded). The **prior** is a Gaussian mixture over a few hundred such training images (each a component with small isotropic spread), so the posterior given a measurement is **exact** (see the math). Measurement: *blur* (Gaussian PSF width) + *noise*, or *missing pixels* (a painted mask: inpainting). Both are diagonal (Fourier domain or pixel domain), so the posterior is cheap. Panels: truth (toggle, "you never have this"), measurement, **classical** restoration (Wiener, as on day 4), **posterior mean**, **four posterior samples** (seeded; *resample*), **disagreement** map (pixel std over 32 samples), and a bar chart of the posterior weight of the top training images ("what the prior thinks this is"). Readouts under each restoration: number of nucleoli, area, protrusion yes/no, PSNR against truth. *Prior* menu: *matched* (trained on all kinds) / *no dividing cells* / *no protrusions* (a prior that has never seen the phenotype). *Truth* menu: from the training distribution, or **unusual** (three nucleoli, a ring-shaped nucleus). Presets: "mild blur: everything agrees", "heavy blur: each sample invents its own nucleoli", "mean is blurry but honest, samples are sharp and disagree", "prior without dividing cells: the division disappears", "unusual nucleus: corrected to a normal one", "inpainting: a protrusion appears in the hole", "PSNR is high, the count is wrong".
- By hand: (a) one forward step: $x_0 = 2$, $\sqrt{\bar\alpha} = 0.8$, $\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha} = 0.6$, $\varepsilon = -1$ gives $x_t = 1$; what $\varepsilon$ should a perfect denoiser report?; (b) data at $\pm1$ with equal probability, $x_t = 0.6\,x_0 + 0.8\,\varepsilon$, observed $x_t = 0.5$: the best denoised guess is $\tanh(0.6\cdot0.5/0.64) \approx 0.44$, neither $+1$ nor $-1$; what does a sampler do instead?; (c) a prior of two training images (a cell with one nucleolus, one with two), a measurement equally compatible with both: posterior mean, the two possible samples, the probability that a sample shows the wrong count; (d) given restored images and disagreement maps, decide for four measurements (area, nucleolus count, mean intensity, protrusion length) whether they may be reported. *(math)*: derive the mean and variance of $\mathbf x_t$ given $\mathbf x_0$ and that $\mathbf x_t \to \mathcal N(0, I)$; derive the 1D two-point posterior mean (the $\tanh$); derive the posterior of a scalar Gaussian prior and Gaussian measurement and show that a sample has twice the expected squared error of the mean; show Tweedie's formula for a Gaussian prior.

### 5. (optional / further reading) `learned-restoration/`: Learned denoising and restoration, and how to check it

- Supervised restoration with paired low/high-quality images (train on pairs from the same sample); trained with squared error it learns the **posterior mean** (blurry where uncertain), with a generative/adversarial loss it learns to **sample** (sharp, may invent): the unit-4 distinction on real methods.
- Self-supervised denoising without clean images: two noisy copies (the noise averages out of the loss), or a **blind spot** (predict a pixel from its neighbours only; works when noise is independent between pixels and fails on correlated noise, e.g. after demosaicing; recall day 3).
- Checks: restore an image with known truth (synthetic or a high-SNR acquisition), compare measurements, not only PSNR; ensembles and disagreement maps; leaving out a phenotype from training to see whether it survives.
- **Demo**: reuse `genrestore` with `data-mode="denoise"` (prior mean vs sample under pure noise) and a blind-spot toy on a 1D signal with independent vs correlated noise (exact linear predictor, no training).
- By hand: why does a blind-spot predictor return the mean of the neighbours for independent noise; what goes wrong when neighbouring noise values are correlated.

## Shared engineering

- **No runtime network access, no model zoo.** Every demo is either analytic (`instancemaps` simulated output, `nmsboxes`, `selfattn`, `calibration`, `genrestore`, `diffusion2d` exact score) or uses a **tiny precomputed model** stored in the bundle (`embedprobe` encoder, `diffusion2d` learned score, optional `instancemaps` real-network set). Training scripts are committed next to each demo for provenance, as `vaeimages/train_shapes.py` is: fixed seed, synthetic or credited data, minutes on a CPU, header comment with the exact command, data and architecture. Any deep-learning framework is named only in these teacher scripts.
- **Precomputed weights and how they are trained offline:**
  - `diffusion2d/train_score.ts`: a node script using `lib/nn.ts` (no Python needed): MLP $(x, y, \text{time features}) \to 2$, about 3 × 64 hidden units (~9k weights), trained by denoising score matching on samples from each preset mixture, fixed seed; two snapshots (under-trained, trained) per dataset; stored as float32 base64 in `score_weights.ts` (~200 KB for all presets; int8 if size matters). Needs `nn.ts` extended with tanh/SiLU and a mini-batch loop (the NN plan proposes those extensions; coordinate, keep `nnfit`/`linae` bit-identical).
  - `embedprobe/train_ssl.py`: Python + framework, as `train_shapes.py`. Encoder: 3 conv layers (8–16–16 channels, stride 2 once) + 1×1 projection to 16-D, ~20k weights. Contrastive training on random 32×32 crops of (a) the shared synthetic generator's images (written to `.u8` by a node script, as the NN plan does for `unetseg`) and (b) `cell256.png` (R. M. Hunt, CC BY 3.0; credited), with augmentations rotation/flip/blur/brightness/noise and **not** hue (documented, it is an exercise question). Optional second weight set with masked-patch reconstruction. Also writes the **label-efficiency curve** (scratch / frozen probe / fine-tuned vs number of labelled crops, mean of 5 seeds) as JSON for a static SVG figure in the lecture, and reference embeddings for the test. Weights int8 with per-channel scale (`Q8Layer` format), inference via `lib/cnn.ts`.
  - Optional `instancemaps/train_instnet.py`: the `unetseg` architecture (NN plan) with four heads (3-class, distance, star-convex rays, flow), trained on the shared generator; int8; only if the "real network" comparison is kept (Open questions).
- **Shared libraries** (reuse first):
  - `lib/cnn.ts`, `lib/q8.ts` (NN plan; `deq` moved out of `vaeimages/imagevae.ts` there, coordinate) for `embedprobe` inference.
  - `lib/imageops.ts` nuclei generator (watershed/NN plans), extended with elongated, bent/C-shaped and tightly touching cells and with nucleoli/protrusion for `genrestore` (or a small `genrestore/cells.ts` if the generator must stay simple).
  - `lib/watershed.ts`, `lib/distance.ts` (watershed plan), `lib/label.ts` (labelling plan) for `instancemaps`.
  - Day 37's `lib/eval.ts` (IoU table, matching at τ, F1, matching accuracy, PR curve and AP) for the readouts in `instancemaps` and `nmsboxes`. `lib/boxes.ts` only adds box geometry; box IoU for evaluation goes through rasterised boxes as label images, or `lib/eval.ts` gains a box-input entry point (to agree with the evaluation plan).
  - `fourier2d/fft.ts` for the Fourier-diagonal blur posterior in `genrestore` (32×32: trivial cost).
  - New `lib/boxes.ts`: box IoU, polygon IoU (convex clipping not enough for star-convex polygons: use rasterised IoU on the 128×128 grid, simple and exact enough), greedy NMS with a step record for the demo.
  - New `lib/mixture.ts`: Gaussian-mixture density, responsibilities, score of the noised mixture, exact posterior under a linear measurement that is diagonal in a given basis (pixel or Fourier). Used by `diffusion2d` and `genrestore`; the GMM day's code (`em2d`) can share the density/responsibility part.
- **Performance** (TypeScript first; measure before Wasm): `instancemaps` flow following, 128² pixels × ≤ 100 Euler steps ≈ 1.6 M updates per redraw (a few ms); heat-diffusion flow targets are computed once per image (Jacobi iterations per cell, ~20 ms, at load). No Wasm expected.
