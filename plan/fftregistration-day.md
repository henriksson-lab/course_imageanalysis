## Goal of the day

After the day a student can:

1. state the **registration problem** for translations (overlapping microscope tiles, drift between time-lapse frames, offset colour channels), score a candidate shift with **SSD**, **cross-correlation** (CC) and **normalised cross-correlation** (NCC), and explain why CC alone is fooled by bright regions;
2. count the cost of a **brute-force search** over shifts, $\mathcal{O}(N\cdot S)$ for $N$ pixels and $S$ shifts ($\approx N^2$ when every shift is allowed), and compare it with the FFT route for realistic sizes;
3. explain why cross-correlation is a **convolution with a flipped image**, and therefore a **product of one spectrum with the conjugate of the other**; compute a short 1D circular cross-correlation by hand both directly and via the DFT;
4. explain **phase correlation**: normalising the cross-power spectrum to unit magnitude turns the correlation into a **sharp peak at the shift** (shift theorem); say why it ignores brightness changes and why it amplifies noise;
5. handle the practical issues: **wrap-around** (circular vs linear correlation, zero-padding), **tapering** (a Hann taper) against edge discontinuities, the **four-way shift ambiguity** of partially overlapping tiles, **sub-pixel** peak estimation, and a **confidence** from the peak;
6. describe how pairwise shifts become a **mosaic** (least squares on a graph of tiles) and how the same tool corrects **drift** and aligns channels and 3D stacks;
7. *(optional, math)* explain how the **Fourier–Mellin** method turns rotation and scale into shifts with a log-polar resampling of the magnitude spectrum.

Why it fits this audience: the day is one theorem from `fourier/` (shift theorem) and one from
the same unit (convolution theorem) put to work, plus an honest cost calculation. The global
stitching step is linear least squares with a graph Laplacian, i.e. their linear algebra.
Everything has a 1D version small enough to do by hand.

### 1. `matching/`: Finding a shift by sliding and scoring

- The problem, with pictures: two tiles of a microscope mosaic overlap by an unknown shift (the stage is not exact); a time-lapse drifts; two colour channels are offset. In all three, **one unknown vector** $(d_x, d_y)$.
- Idea: try every shift, slide one image over the other, **score** the overlap, keep the best. Picture: a 1D profile sliding over another and the score curve below it.
- Three scores, in words first: **SSD** (sum of squared differences: small is good), **cross-correlation** (sum of products: large is good), **NCC** (correlation after removing mean and dividing by spread on the overlap: between −1 and 1). CC is fooled by bright regions (a bright blob scores high wherever it lands); SSD is fooled by a brightness change; NCC by neither. Each failure shown in the demo.
- **The math: three scores** {.math}: $\mathrm{SSD}(\mathbf d) = \sum_{\mathbf x}(f(\mathbf x) - g(\mathbf x + \mathbf d))^2 = \sum f^2 + \sum g^2 - 2\,\mathrm{CC}(\mathbf d)$ over the overlap: SSD and CC differ only by energies, which change with $\mathbf d$ when images are uneven. NCC $= \frac{\sum (f - \bar f)(g - \bar g)}{\sqrt{\sum (f - \bar f)^2 \sum (g - \bar g)^2}}$ = cosine of the angle between the two mean-free overlap vectors (Cauchy–Schwarz gives $|{\rm NCC}| \le 1$); invariant to $g \mapsto ag + b$, $a > 0$.
- **Cost**: $S$ shifts × $N$ pixels each. Worked numbers on a slide: two 1024 × 1024 tiles with every shift allowed: $\sum_{d}(N_x - |d_x|)(N_y - |d_y|) = N^2 \cdot N^2 \approx 10^{12}$ multiply-adds (minutes to hours). With a known search window of ±32 px: $65^2 \cdot 10^6 \approx 4\cdot 10^9$ (seconds). The question for unit 2: can we get all shifts at once, cheaply?
- **Template matching** is the same machine with a small image: NCC map of a template (a nucleus) over the micrograph, peaks = occurrences. One slide; pointers to the Hough elective (voting) and, for the denominators, the integral-images elective.
- Contrast slide: intensity-based vs landmark-based alignment (Procrustes, snakes elective).
- **Demo `xcorr1d`** (new; `data-mode="direct"` in this unit): two 1D signals (a line profile across `cell256.png`, synthetic bumps, a step, a periodic signal); drag signal $g$ over $f$ or use a shift slider; the overlap is shaded; below, the score curve (SSD / CC / NCC, selectable, or all three normalised to their range) being drawn as you slide, with the current shift marked and the best shift reported. Controls: gain and offset on $g$, a brightness ramp on $g$, noise, overlap length. A readout counts multiply-adds so far. Presets: "a matching bump", "brighter copy: SSD fails, NCC doesn't", "a bright blob elsewhere: CC peaks in the wrong place", "periodic signal: many equal peaks", "tiny overlap: NCC is noisy at the ends".
- **Demo `xcorr2d`/template mode**: use `phasecorr` (unit 3) with `data-method="ncc" data-template="1"`: a draggable template box on the micrograph, the NCC map, top peaks marked. (No separate demo.)
- By hand: template $t = (1, 3, 1)$ against $s = (0, 1, 3, 1, 0, 2, 6, 2)$ at all 6 positions. CC is largest at the brighter copy (22 vs 11); SSD picks the exact copy (0 vs 11); NCC scores both copies 1. Which score would you use for a mosaic whose tiles differ in exposure? Then a cost estimate for 2048 × 2048 tiles with a 10 % overlap.

### 2. `xcorr-fft/`: Cross-correlation with the FFT

- Recall in one line each: correlation = convolution with the kernel flipped (`kernels/`); convolution = product of spectra (`fourier/`); the FFT costs $\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$.
- Concept: flipping an image in space corresponds to **conjugating** its spectrum (for real images). So all correlation values at once = inverse FFT of $\overline{F}\cdot G$: two forward FFTs, one product, one inverse FFT. Picture: the pipeline as a diagram, with each spectrum shown as an image.
- Intuition for the product, without formulas: each frequency is a wave in both images; $\overline{F_k}G_k$ records **how far the wave in $g$ is shifted relative to the wave in $f$** (the phase difference) and how strong the wave is in both; the inverse FFT adds up all waves, and they line up at the true shift.
- **The math: the correlation theorem** {.math}: $c(\mathbf d) = \sum_{\mathbf x} f(\mathbf x)\,g(\mathbf x + \mathbf d)$ (indices modulo $N$); $\hat c_{\mathbf k} = \overline{F_{\mathbf k}}\,G_{\mathbf k}$ for real $f$. Proof in three lines from the DFT definition (or from the convolution theorem with $f^-(\mathbf x) = f(-\mathbf x)$ and $\widehat{f^-} = \overline{F}$).
- **Circular vs linear**: the DFT treats both images as periodic, so a shift pushes $g$ out on one side and in on the other: the correlation is **circular** (the "wrap" border rule). For images that only partly overlap that is wrong: **zero-pad** each image to at least $2N - 1$ (in practice the next power of two, $2N$) to get the linear correlation. Picture: the 1D signal and its periodic copies.
- **Cost, worked**: 1024 × 1024 tiles padded to 2048²: one complex FFT ≈ $5M\log_2 M$ flops with $M = 2048^2 \approx 4.2\cdot10^6$, i.e. $\approx 4.6\cdot10^8$; three FFTs ≈ $1.4\cdot10^9$ flops vs $\approx 2\cdot10^{12}$ for brute force: **about 1000× faster**, milliseconds instead of minutes. But for a ±32 px search window brute force (≈ $8\cdot10^9$ flops) is in the same range: the FFT wins when the shift is **unknown**, not always. The demo measures both.
- NCC via FFT: the numerator is a correlation (FFT); the local sums in the denominator are box-filter correlations (FFT again, or integral images: integral-images elective). Stated, one slide, math slide gives the formula.
- **Demo `xcorr1d`** with `data-mode="fft"`: same signals as unit 1; extra panels: $|F|$, $|G|$, the phase difference per frequency as arrows on unit circles (one per frequency, up to $N = 32$), the product, and its inverse FFT drawn **on top of** the brute-force curve (they coincide in circular mode). Toggle *zero-pad*: circular vs linear curve, and the wrong peak from wrap-around disappears. A cost panel: log–log operation counts (brute force full, brute force window, FFT) against $N$ with the current $N$ marked. Presets: "the curves agree", "wrap-around fools the circular correlation", "padding fixes it", "the waves line up at the shift".
- Timing in 2D lives in the `phasecorr` demo (unit 3): a "run brute force once" button with measured times for both routes on the 256² crops.
- By hand: $f = (1, 2, 0, 0)$, $g = (0, 1, 2, 0)$ ($f$ shifted right by one). (a) Circular CC directly: $c = (2, 5, 2, 0)$ for $d = 0..3$. (b) Via the DFT: $F = (3, 1 - 2i, -1, 1 + 2i)$, $G = (3, -2 - i, 1, -2 + i)$, $\overline F G = (9, -5i, -1, 5i)$, inverse DFT gives the same $(2, 5, 2, 0)$. Uses the length-4 DFT of `fourier/` B7 (powers of $-i$). (c) Concept: what changes if $g$ is shifted by 3 instead of 1 and not padded?
- Math questions: prove the correlation theorem; show the cross-power of a circularly shifted copy is $|F_k|^2 e^{-2\pi i k d/N}$ (using B9); count flops for padding to $2N$ vs $N$.

### 3. `phasecorr/`: Phase correlation

- Problem with plain CC (shown in the demo first): the peak is **broad** (images are dominated by slow waves), and it moves with uneven brightness.
- Idea: in $\overline{F_k}G_k$ only the **phase difference** carries the shift (shift theorem, `fourier/` B4: a shift changes phases, not sizes). So throw away the sizes: divide each frequency by its magnitude. Every wave then votes with weight 1, all votes agree at the true shift, and the inverse FFT is a **single sharp peak** (a delta for a pure circular shift). Picture: arrows on unit circles all rotating at the rate set by the shift.
- **The math: why a delta** {.math}: $g(\mathbf x) = f(\mathbf x - \mathbf d)$ ⇒ $G_{\mathbf k} = e^{-2\pi i\,\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf d/N}F_{\mathbf k}$ (B9) ⇒ $R_{\mathbf k} = \frac{\overline{F_{\mathbf k}}G_{\mathbf k}}{|\overline{F_{\mathbf k}}G_{\mathbf k}|} = e^{-2\pi i\,\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf d/N}$ ⇒ $\mathcal F^{-1}R = \delta(\mathbf x - \mathbf d)$. Kuglin & Hines (1975).
- What it buys: (a) **brightness**: gain and offset (offset lives only in $k = 0$) do not change the phases: invariant; slow illumination gradients mostly affect low frequencies, which now weigh no more than others. (b) A **sharp** peak, easy to locate, and two shifts present at once (two moving objects) give two peaks. In words: phase correlation compares **edges and texture**, CC compares **blobs of brightness** (normalising the spectrum = whitening the images = an edge-enhancing filter).
- What it costs: **noise**. High frequencies are mostly noise, and now weigh as much as the signal frequencies: the peak sinks into a noisy surface. Remedies shown in the demo: low-pass weighting (keep the normalisation, then multiply by a smooth low-pass), partial normalisation $|\cdot|^p$ with $0 < p < 1$ (p = 0 is CC, p = 1 is phase correlation), $\varepsilon$ in the denominator (also needed where $|F_k| \approx 0$; compute in floating point, day 3). Noise as $\sigma_n$, recalled from `cameranoise/` (day 3).
- **Tapering**: the periodic assumption puts a jump at the image border (recall `fourier/`, day 1: the DFT assumes the image repeats, so it leaks), and the border's jump lines are shared by both images and **do not move** with the shift: a strong false peak at $(0, 0)$ and a bright cross in the spectrum. Multiply both images by a **Hann taper** (a smooth bump that is 0 at the border) before the FFT. Cost: the overlap's edges count less. Picture: spectrum with and without the cross.
- **The math: tapers and the cross-power with noise** {.math}: Hann $w(n) = \tfrac12(1 - \cos(2\pi n/(N-1)))$, separable in 2D; with $g = f(\cdot - \mathbf d) + \eta$, $R_{\mathbf k}$ has phase error ≈ $|\eta_{\mathbf k}|/|F_{\mathbf k}|$, large exactly where $|F_{\mathbf k}|$ is small (high $\mathbf k$): why weighting helps.
- **Confidence**: for a pure shift the peak height is 1; for partial overlap it is roughly the shared fraction of the content; compare the highest peak with the second highest (peak ratio). Low ratio = do not trust this pair (blank tile, periodic texture).
- **Demo `phasecorr`** (new, main demo): the micrograph `cell256.png` with two draggable crop frames (default 128²; the second frame's offset is the true shift, also set numerically); panels: crop A, crop B (after the selected degradations), the spectrum of A (log magnitude) or the phase of the cross-power as hue, and the **correlation surface** as a heat map (optional WebGL height map as a `lib/gl3d.ts` height-field primitive; the heat map is the default and the print fallback). Readout: detected shift, true shift, error, peak height, peak ratio, time for the FFT route and (button) for brute force. Controls: method CC / NCC / phase, exponent $p$ slider, low-pass weight radius, noise σ, gain and offset on B, illumination ramp on B, Hann taper on/off, zero-padding on/off. Presets: "same image, shifted: delta", "CC: broad peak", "illumination ramp: CC wrong, phase right", "noise: phase peak drowns; low-pass weighting rescues", "no taper: the cross and the false (0, 0) peak", "periodic texture: two peaks of equal height" (synthetic image), "template mode: find all nuclei" (NCC). Optional 3D tab `data-dim="3"`: a synthetic 64³ volume of seeded blobs and a shifted noisy copy, the correlation volume shown as three MIPs (xy, xz, yz) with the peak marked: no WebGL needed.
- By hand: repeat the unit 2 example with normalisation: $R = (1, -i, -1, i)$, inverse DFT $(0, 1, 0, 0)$: a delta at $d = 1$. Then $g' = 2g + 1$: show that $R$ is unchanged (only $G_0$ changes, and it is normalised to 1). Concept: predict the correlation surface of two images that share only a strong straight edge.
- Math questions: phase correlation of a sub-pixel shift (1D) is the Dirichlet kernel (periodic sinc) centred at the true shift (prepares unit 4); show that $p = 0$ gives CC and that phase correlation equals CC of the whitened images.

### 4. `stitching/`: From a peak to a mosaic: ambiguity, sub-pixel, global alignment, drift

- **Four-way ambiguity**: without padding, a peak at $(p, q)$ only says the shift modulo the tile size: $d_x \in \{p, p - N\}$, $d_y \in \{q, q - N\}$, four candidates. Picture: the four ways tile B can sit against tile A. Resolve by computing NCC on the **actual overlap** of each candidate and keeping the best (with a minimum overlap, since tiny overlaps give unreliable NCC). Padding avoids the ambiguity but halves the peak sharpness and costs 4× the FFT size; stitching tools usually test candidates instead.
- **Sub-pixel**: shifts are rarely whole pixels, and drift of 0.3 px matters for localisation microscopy. (a) **Parabolic fit** through the peak and its two neighbours in each direction: $\delta = \frac{c_{-1} - c_{+1}}{2(c_{-1} - 2c_0 + c_{+1})}$; biased towards integers ("pixel locking"), shown in the demo. (b) **Upsampled DFT** around the peak: evaluate the inverse DFT of $R$ only on a fine grid (e.g. 1/20 px) in a 1.5 × 1.5 px neighbourhood, by a small matrix product instead of a huge padded FFT (Guizar-Sicairos et al. 2008). (c) Centroid of the peak, as the simplest baseline. Applying a sub-pixel shift needs interpolation (`images/`) or a phase ramp in Fourier space (shift theorem backwards).
- **The math: sub-pixel peaks** {.math}: parabola derivation (fit $a\delta^2 + b\delta + c$ through three points, vertex at $-b/2a$); the phase-correlation peak for a non-integer shift is a Dirichlet kernel (unit 3 math question), so an analytic fit exists (Foroosh et al. 2002); the upsampled DFT as $\mathbf E_y\,R\,\mathbf E_x^\top$ with small DFT matrices, cost $\mathcal{O}(N \cdot U)$ for $U$ upsampled points.
- **Global alignment of a mosaic**: pairwise shifts $\mathbf d_{ij}$ between neighbouring tiles do not add up exactly around a loop (noise, a bad pair). Picture: 3 × 3 tiles as a graph, edges = pairwise shifts with confidence. Find tile positions that agree best with all edges: weighted **least squares**, one tile fixed. Bad edges (low confidence, large residual) are dropped, or a maximum spanning tree on confidence is used (as in MIST). Blending of seams: one slide (feathering), with vignetting correction as a pointer.
- **The math: least squares on a graph** {.math}: minimise $\sum_{(i,j)} w_{ij}\,\lVert \mathbf x_j - \mathbf x_i - \mathbf d_{ij}\rVert^2$; normal equations $L\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ with $L$ the weighted graph Laplacian (forward pointer to day 13, `ncut/`), singular by exactly the global translation, fixed by pinning $\mathbf x_1$; per axis separately.
- **Drift and other applications** (one slide each, with a picture): time-lapse drift (register each frame to the first or to the previous one; to the previous accumulates a random-walk error, to the first fails when the sample changes: compromise by key frames); single-molecule localisation (no images, but localisations rendered into images per time block; correlate all block pairs and solve the same least-squares graph problem, redundant cross-correlation; fiducial beads as the alternative); **channel alignment** (honest: channels show different structures, so correlate a bead/calibration image, and chromatic aberration also scales, so a pure shift is only a first approximation); **3D stacks**: same formula with a 3D FFT, report the shift in µm using the voxel spacing (recall `volumes3d/`, marching-cubes elective); motion estimation (block matching per window: particle image velocimetry, video coding): one line each.
- Limits: translation only (rotation/scale: unit 5), rigid only (non-rigid registration, optical flow and tracking: the Motion elective, pointer only; feature-based registration with interest points: day 2 `structure-tensor/` + Motion elective), repeated textures and empty tiles.
- **Demo `stitch`** (new): a 3 × 3 mosaic of 96² tiles cut from the (upsampled to 384²) micrograph with 16–24 px overlaps, true positions = nominal grid + seeded jitter of a few pixels; per-tile noise and brightness/vignetting. Views: the mosaic at nominal (stage) positions with visible seams; the tile graph with edges coloured by peak ratio; click an edge to see that pair's phase-correlation surface and the **four candidates** with their overlap NCC; buttons *pairwise* (chain along a spanning tree), *global least squares* (residual per edge shown), *drop worst edge*, *blend*. Readout: position error per tile vs truth. Presets: "nominal positions: seams", "pairwise along a row: errors accumulate", "global: errors spread", "an empty tile: low-confidence edges, global rescues it", "a wrong pair: its residual stands out", "the four candidates".
- **Demo `subpixel`** (new, small): 1D (and a 2D tab) signal and a copy shifted by a fractional amount through the Fourier phase ramp (so the truth is exact); correlation samples near the peak with the parabola fit, centroid, and upsampled-DFT curve; a sweep plot of estimated − true against the true fraction 0…1 for all three methods (pixel locking of the parabola visible as an S-curve); sliders: fractional shift, noise, upsampling factor, CC vs phase. Presets: "parabola: pixel locking", "upsampled DFT: flat error", "noise sets the floor".
- **Demo `drift`** (new, small): 40 frames made from the micrograph with a seeded random-walk drift, noise and bleaching (falling brightness); register each to the first / to the previous frame / to key frames; plot of estimated vs true trajectory, average image before and after correction (blurred vs sharp). ### 5. (optional / further reading) `fourier-mellin/`: Rotation and scale

- The magnitude spectrum $|F|$ ignores shifts (`fourier/` B4). Rotating the image rotates $|F|$ by the same angle; scaling the image by $s$ scales $|F|$ by $1/s$. Resample $|F|$ on a **log-polar** grid ($\log r$, $\theta$): rotation and scale become **shifts**, which phase correlation finds. Undo them, then phase correlate again for the translation (Reddy & Chatterji 1996; De Castro & Morandi 1987 for rotation).
- Practicalities: the 180° ambiguity ($|F|$ of a real image is point-symmetric, so test $\theta$ and $\theta + 180°$); high-pass weighting of $|F|$ (low frequencies dominate and carry little angle information); tapering is essential (the border cross does not rotate); log-polar resampling needs interpolation (`images/`); usable range of scale ≈ 0.5–2.
- **The math: Fourier–Mellin** {.math}: $g(\mathbf x) = f(sR\mathbf x + \mathbf t)$ ⇒ $|G(\mathbf k)| = s^{-2}|F(s^{-1}R\mathbf k)|$ (continuous FT); in log-polar coordinates $(\rho, \theta) = (\log|\mathbf k|, \arg\mathbf k)$: $|G|(\rho, \theta) = s^{-2}|F|(\rho - \log s, \theta - \alpha)$: a shift. The Mellin transform is the Fourier transform in $\log r$.
- **Demo `fmellin`** (new): image B = A rotated / scaled / shifted (sliders) with noise; panels: $|F_A|$, $|F_B|$, their log-polar resamplings, the phase-correlation surface in $(\log r, \theta)$ with the peak → angle and scale, then the translation step and the overlay result. Toggles: Hann taper, high-pass on $|F|$, both 180° candidates. Presets: "pure rotation 30°", "scale 1.5 + shift", "180° ambiguity", "no taper: the cross dominates", "scale 3: out of range".
- By hand: a 30° rotation moves the log-polar peak by how many $\theta$ bins (256 bins over 180°)? A scaling by 2 with 64 $\log r$ bins spanning $r = 1 \ldots 128$: shift $= \ln 2 / (\ln 128 / 64) \approx 9.1$ bins.

Budget for live interaction (< 30 ms): three Float64 FFTs of 256² are a few ms; with padding to 512² ≈ 15–30 ms, borderline, so measure, and consider Float32 or Wasm only if needed.
