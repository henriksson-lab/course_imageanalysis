## Goal of the day

After the day a student can:

1. explain why colour is **three numbers**: a spectrum is (almost) infinite-dimensional, and the eye or a camera applies a **linear map** to $\mathbb{R}^3$ (three cone types, or three filters); compute cone responses for a small sampled spectrum, and construct a **metamer** from the null space of that map;
2. read a **CIE xy chromaticity diagram**: the spectral locus, the white point, why mixtures lie on straight lines, and why a display's **gamut** is a triangle;
3. convert between **linear and gamma-encoded sRGB**, explain why blurring, resizing and averaging must be done in linear light, and build the **3×3 matrix** from a display's primaries and white point to CIE XYZ (and the matrices to YCbCr and luma);
4. compute **HSV** and **CIELAB** coordinates of a colour, explain where HSV is singular (grey) and why hue is an angle, use **ΔE** in Lab as a colour difference, and choose a space in which a colour **segmentation** becomes a simple threshold;
5. correct the colour of an image as a linear map: **white balance** (von Kries diagonal scaling in linear light, grey world, white patch), and say when it fails;
6. **display** data with colour: pseudocolour and channel merges, sequential/diverging/qualitative **colour maps** with monotone lightness, checking figures for colour-blind viewers, and a decorrelation stretch as PCA of the colour cloud.

Why it fits this audience: nearly every step is linear algebra they already own.
Colour vision is a rank-3 linear map with a large null space (metamers), a change of
primaries is a change of basis, white balance is a diagonal matrix in a well-chosen
basis, and a decorrelation stretch is PCA of the colour cloud. The non-linear
parts (gamma, HSV, Lab) are single-variable functions whose shape can be drawn. The
spectrum integrals $\int s(\lambda)\,\bar{x}(\lambda)\,d\lambda$ become sums the moment
the spectrum is sampled, which is how the demos and the by-hand exercises treat them.

### 1. `colour-vision/`: From a spectrum to three numbers

- Motivation: two lamps that look the same white but make a red apple look different; a camera that sees two fabrics as different colours while we see them as equal. Colour is not a property of light alone.
- **Light is a spectrum** $s(\lambda)$, roughly 380–730 nm: picture of daylight, an incandescent bulb (Planck), a white LED (blue peak + phosphor hump), a laser line. Reflectance $r(\lambda)$ of a surface; what reaches the eye is $s(\lambda)\,r(\lambda)$.
- **Three cone types** L, M, S with broad overlapping sensitivities $\bar{l}, \bar{m}, \bar{s}$. Response $L = \int s(\lambda)\,\bar{l}(\lambda)\,d\lambda$, etc. Sample at 10 nm: $s \in \mathbb{R}^{36}$, and $\mathbf{c} = A\mathbf{s}$ with $A \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 36}$. **Colour vision is a linear map of rank 3.** A camera is the same with its own $A_{\text{cam}}$.
- **Metamers**: $A\mathbf{s}_1 = A\mathbf{s}_2$ whenever $\mathbf{s}_2 - \mathbf{s}_1 \in \ker A$, a 33-dimensional subspace. Constraint: $\mathbf{s}_2 \ge 0$ (a physical spectrum). Why displays work at all: three primaries can produce a metamer of (many) real spectra. Why they fail: an eye–camera mismatch ($\ker A \ne \ker A_{\text{cam}}$), and an illuminant change can break a metameric match (metameric failure; links to white balance in unit 2).
- Colour blindness in one slide: a dichromat has rank 2 (one cone type missing), so more pairs are metamers. (Simulation as a projection: optional unit.)
- **Colour matching** (Wright–Guild): a test light matched by three primaries; some matches need **negative** amounts (the primary added to the test side). The colour matching functions $\bar{r}, \bar{g}, \bar{b}$ are a linear combination of the cone fundamentals (another basis of the same 3D row space).
- **CIE XYZ** (1931): a change of basis chosen so that $\bar{x}, \bar{y}, \bar{z} \ge 0$ everywhere and $\bar{y}$ = luminous efficiency, so $Y$ = **luminance**. All real colours have $X, Y, Z \ge 0$; the XYZ "primaries" themselves are not physical.
- **Chromaticity**: $x = X/(X+Y+Z)$, $y = Y/(X+Y+Z)$: central projection onto the plane $X+Y+Z = 1$ (picture), forgetting brightness. The **spectral locus** (monochromatic lights) is the horseshoe; the purple line closes it. Additive mixtures lie on the **straight line** between the two chromaticities (the projection maps lines to lines), so a display with three primaries reaches exactly the **triangle** between them: its **gamut**. sRGB, Display P3, Rec. 2020 compared. The white point (D65) inside.
- Honest caveat on the projector: the slides cannot show colours outside the projector's gamut; the horseshoe is drawn with out-of-gamut colours clipped and marked.
- **Demo `spectrum`** (new, main demo of the unit): top, a spectrum on a 380–730 nm axis, edited by dragging ~12 control points (smooth interpolation) or chosen from presets (daylight D65, blackbody with temperature slider, white LED, three laser lines, fluorescent tube with spikes). Toggle *observer: eye / camera / dichromat*: the same pair is then no longer a match (two swatches split). Toggle *illuminant*: multiply both by a lamp spectrum (the pair as reflectances): the match breaks under the other lamp. ### 2. `rgb-linear/`: RGB as a device space: gamma, linear maps and white balance

- **RGB is a device space**: "(255, 0, 0)" means "full drive of this display's red primary". Without primaries and a white point it is not a colour. **sRGB** (IEC 61966-2-1) fixes primaries (the unit 1 triangle), white D65 and a transfer function; it is what an untagged 8-bit image and the browser canvas assume.
- Recall day 3 (`highbit/`): "you saw sRGB storage codes; here is why they exist".
- **Gamma encoding**: perceived lightness is roughly a power ~1/2.4–1/3 of luminance, so 8 bits are spent better on encoded values. sRGB transfer: $C_{\text{lin}} = C/12.92$ for $C \le 0.04045$, else $\big((C + 0.055)/1.055\big)^{2.4}$; inverse for encoding. Picture: the curve, and an 8-bit linear ramp vs encoded ramp (banding in the darks). Camera raw data and microscope detectors are **linear**; JPEG photographs are **encoded**.
- **Why it matters for image analysis**: blur, resampling, averaging, mixing and photometric measurement are linear operations on **light**. Done on encoded values they are wrong: the average of black (0) and white (255) is 128 in encoded values, which is only 21.6 % of white's luminance; the correct mid-grey of the light is linear 0.5, encoded **188**. Consequences: dark halos when blurring bright details, wrong colours where red meets green (a muddy dark band), images that darken when downsampled, wrong intensity ratios. In quantitative microscopy: never measure on gamma-encoded exports.
- **Linear maps between spaces**: in linear RGB, a colour is $\mathbf{c} = R\,\mathbf{p}_R + G\,\mathbf{p}_G + B\,\mathbf{p}_B$, so $\mathbf{XYZ} = M\,\mathbf{rgb}$ with the primaries' XYZ as columns. The columns are scaled so that $M\,(1,1,1)^\top$ = white point: given chromaticities of the primaries and the white, solve one $3 \times 3$ system for the three scales. sRGB → XYZ:
  $M = \begin{pmatrix} 0.4124 & 0.3576 & 0.1805 \\ 0.2126 & 0.7152 & 0.0722 \\ 0.0193 & 0.1192 & 0.9505 \end{pmatrix}$.
  The middle row is the **luminance** weights. RGB of display A → RGB of display B $= M_B^{-1} M_A$: a change of basis. Out-of-gamut colours give components $< 0$ or $> 1$; clipping vs gamut mapping in one slide.
- **Converting to greyscale** (picture: one photo, four greys): luminance $Y = 0.2126R + 0.7152G + 0.0722B$ in **linear** RGB; the video-standard **luma** $Y' = 0.299R' + 0.587G' + 0.114B'$ on encoded values (an approximation); the channel mean (treats the eye as equally sensitive to blue); a single channel (often right for fluorescence or stained tissue: pick the channel with the contrast). An isoluminant test image (red text on green of equal luminance) disappears under luminance but not under the mean: "greyscale" is a choice, not a fact.
- **Opponent spaces and YCbCr**: the visual system recodes cone signals into an achromatic and two opponent channels (red–green, blue–yellow). **YCbCr** (JPEG, full range): $Y' = 0.299R' + 0.587G' + 0.114B'$, $C_b = 128 + 0.564(B' - Y')$, $C_r = 128 + 0.713(R' - Y')$: a $3 \times 3$ matrix plus an offset. **Chroma subsampling** (4:2:0): store $C_b, C_r$ at half resolution in both directions; the image looks unchanged, while subsampling $Y'$ the same way is obvious. Recall from `fourier/`: the eye's colour-difference channels are low-pass.
- **White balance** (moved here from the old unit 4: it is a diagonal matrix in linear RGB). **Colour constancy**: the camera sees $\mathbf{c} = A_{\text{cam}}\,\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s})\,\mathbf{r}$ (illuminant × reflectance); we want the colour under D65. **von Kries** (1902): $\mathbf{c}' = D\,\mathbf{c}$ with $D = \operatorname{diag}(w_\text{ref}/w)$, applied to **linear** values; better in a sharpened cone basis (Bradford: $M^{-1} D M$, one slide). Estimating the white: **grey world**, **white patch / max-RGB**, a **clicked grey card**; failure modes (a forest turned grey-magenta, a clipped highlight). In microscopy: brightfield white reference per channel is a per-pixel white balance (flat-field: pointer to day 4 / `cameranoise/`).
- Forward pointer (one slide): brightfield stains absorb, so in **optical density** $-\log_{10}(I/I_0)$ two stains add linearly; separating them is the unmixing elective (`stain-unmixing/`).
- **Demo `gammablur`** (new): an image and a Gaussian blur / downsample, computed twice: on encoded values (naive) and in linear light (decode → blur → encode). Side by side with a difference image and a draggable 1D profile showing both curves. Controls: $\sigma$, *blur / downsample ×2, ×4 / mix two colours*, a toggle to show linear values directly (looks too dark: why we encode). Inputs: thin white lines on black, a red/green checker, "lights at night" (synthetic bright points on dark, seeded), a colour photo (see open questions), a fluorescence-like image. Presets: "black + white = 128 or 188?" (with a fine stripe pattern next to two uniform patches, to compare by squinting, with a caveat that the projector may not follow sRGB), "red meets green: the dark seam", "blurred lights lose their glow", "downsampling darkens fine detail".
- **Demo `colourmatrix`** (new): a colour image, a $3 \times 3$ matrix (editable cells, with offset) and the transformed channels shown as three grey images plus the recombined colour image. Modes (`data-mode`): *sRGB→XYZ*, *YCbCr* (with chroma subsampling factor 1/2/4/8 for $C_bC_r$ or for $Y'$), *greyscale* (weights sliders constrained to sum to 1, presets luminance / luma / mean / single channel, applied to linear or encoded values), *display A→B* (from the `chromaticity` custom display). Presets: "Y′CbCr channels", "subsample chroma by 4: invisible", "subsample luma by 4: obvious", "the isoluminant test", "greyscale of a stained tissue: the best single channel".
- **Demo `whitebalance`** (new, moved with white balance): a synthetic scene rendered **spectrally** (seeded smooth reflectance spectra for a 4×6 chart of patches + a few objects, times an illuminant spectrum, times a camera or the CIE observer), so the ground truth under D65 is known. Illuminant: blackbody 2500–10000 K slider, fluorescent with spikes, narrow-band RGB LED. Correction: none, grey world, white patch, click a patch, and the space it is applied in (encoded sRGB / linear RGB / LMS von Kries / Bradford), plus a full 3×3 least-squares fit to the chart. Readout: mean and max ΔE over the patches against the D65 rendering (ΔE from unit 3: forward reference, or show the RGB error until then). Presets: "tungsten 2800 K", "grey world works", "grey world in a forest", "white patch fooled by a highlight", "diagonal on encoded values", "narrow-band LED: no diagonal fixes it".
- By hand: (a) decode 0, 128, 188, 255 with the sRGB transfer function; the average of black and white in encoded and in linear values; (b) build the RGB→XYZ matrix of a toy display from given XYZ of its primaries (unscaled) and a white point: solve for the three scales; check the white row sum; (c) $Y'C_bC_r$ of pure red and of mid-grey; (d) count the bytes saved by 4:2:0; (e) grey-world scales for given channel means, and the corrected value of one pixel.

### 3. `colour-perceptual/`: HSV, CIELAB, colour differences and colour segmentation

- The question for analysis: "find the red objects", "how different are these two colours?" Neither is easy in RGB: a shadow changes all three channels, and equal RGB distances are not equal perceived differences.
- **HSV / HSL** (Smith 1978): a re-parametrisation of the RGB cube for picking colours. $V = \max(R,G,B)$, $S = (\max - \min)/\max$, $H$ = angle around the grey diagonal, computed piecewise on a hexagon (one slide, picture of the cube seen along its diagonal). Properties:
  - **Hue is an angle**: red sits at both 0° and 360°, so a hue threshold for red is a union of two intervals, and the mean hue needs the circular mean (average the unit vectors $(\cos H, \sin H)$).
  - **Singular at grey**: for $\max = \min$ the hue is undefined and $S$ is 0/0 at black. Near grey, a tiny noise flips the hue arbitrarily: a hue mask on a noisy image speckles in dark and grey regions; fix with a saturation (and value) floor.
  - Hue and saturation are (nearly) invariant to **shading** (scaling all channels): why a hue threshold survives shadows where an R-channel threshold fails. Same idea: **normalised rgb** $r = R/(R+G+B)$, $g = G/(R+G+B)$, the camera's own chromaticity.
  - HSV is not perceptual and not device-independent: "V" of pure blue and pure yellow are both 1.
- **CIELAB** (1976): $L^* = 116 f(Y/Y_n) - 16$, $a^* = 500\,[f(X/X_n) - f(Y/Y_n)]$, $b^* = 200\,[f(Y/Y_n) - f(Z/Z_n)]$, with $f(t) = t^{1/3}$ above a small threshold and linear below. Read: lightness from a cube root (like gamma), and two **opponent** axes (green–red, blue–yellow) as differences of compressed responses; the white point $(X_n, Y_n, Z_n)$ is built in (a crude white balance). Polar form: chroma $C^*_{ab}$, hue angle $h_{ab}$. CIELUV in one line (similar goal, a chromaticity diagram that is more uniform).
- **ΔE**: $\Delta E^*_{ab}$ = Euclidean distance in Lab; ≈ 1–2.3 is a just-noticeable difference. Lab is only approximately uniform (MacAdam ellipses picture); CIEDE2000 corrects it (formula not taught, used in the demo as a toggle). Contrast with Euclidean distance in sRGB: two pairs with the same RGB distance, one nearly invisible (dark blues), one obvious (greens).
- **Choosing a space for segmentation**: the pixels of an image are a **point cloud in 3D**; a segmentation is a region of that space. A good space makes the region simple: a hue wedge with a saturation floor (HSV), a disc or box in $a^*b^*$, a ball of radius $\Delta E$ around a reference colour (Lab), or two/three clusters (**one slide: isodata from day 5 in 3D = k-means**, run in Lab; the algorithm is owned by the GMM elective). A 2D histogram over the two chromatic axes ($H$–$S$, $a^*$–$b^*$, $C_b$–$C_r$, $r$–$g$) is the picture in which to draw the region; Otsu on a single derived channel (hue, $a^*$) is the one-threshold version (back-reference to day 5, `lib/threshold.ts`).
- Pointer: a per-class colour model (a Gaussian in Lab) is the data term of day 12 (graph cuts, GrabCut).
- **Demo `colourcloud`** (new, main demo of the day, WebGL via `lib/gl3d.ts`): left, a colour image (photo, a synthetic scene of coloured objects with shading and noise, an H&E image); right, its pixels (subsampled to ~20–50k, seeded) as a rotatable 3D point cloud, each point drawn in its own colour, inside the outline of the sRGB gamut in the current space. Spaces: encoded sRGB (cube), linear RGB, XYZ, YCbCr, HSV (cone/cylinder, hue as angle), Lab. *Animate* morphs the cloud continuously from one space to another (interpolated positions), so students see the cube bend into a cone or into the Lab solid. Brushing both ways: paint on the image → the points light up; drag a box / ball / hue wedge in 3D → the pixels light up in the image. Picked pixel: its coordinates in all spaces. Toggles: show the grey axis, show the 2D projection onto the chromatic plane, noise level, shading (multiply the image by a gradient: the cloud stretches along rays through black). Presets: "the RGB cube becomes the HSV cone", "shadows are rays through black", "grey pixels scatter in hue", "Lab: the white point at the top", "an H&E slide: two stains, a 2D sheet" (the unmixing elective adds an OD option). Also used in unit 4 (`data-mode="pca"`).
- **Demo `colourseg`** (new): image (left), 2D histogram of the chosen chromatic pair (middle, log scale), mask (right). Methods: per-channel RGB thresholds, hue interval + saturation and value floors (drawn as a wedge on the $H$–$S$ disc; the interval may wrap through 0°), ΔE ball around a clicked reference colour (radius slider, ΔE76 / ΔE2000, or Euclidean in sRGB for comparison), Otsu on a chosen channel, k-means with $k = 2..5$ in RGB or Lab (seeded initialisation, *step* button). Inputs: synthetic scene with shadows and noise (known ground truth, Dice readout via day 5's evaluation code in `lib/metrics.ts`), colour photo, H&E. Presets: "R-channel threshold fails in the shadow", "hue survives the shadow", "red wraps around 0°", "grey and dark pixels speckle: add a saturation floor", "ΔE ball in Lab vs RGB distance", "k-means in RGB vs in Lab".
- By hand: (a) HSV of (255, 0, 0), (0, 128, 0), (200, 100, 50), (128, 128, 128) (the last: hue undefined); (b) the circular mean of hues 350° and 10° vs the arithmetic mean; (c) $L^*$ of 18 % grey ($Y = 0.18$: $L^* \approx 50$, why "middle grey" is not 50 % luminance); (d) ΔE between two given Lab colours, and for two given pairs, which differs more in Lab and which in sRGB.

### 4. `colour-display/`: Pseudocolour, colour maps and decorrelation (now core)

Was optional unit 5; made core (review-organisation §2.8: every demo in the course uses its
colour maps). Stain deconvolution, formerly unit 4 `colour-unmixing/`, moved to the
unmixing elective as `stain-unmixing/`.

- Recall (one line): window/level and LUTs for high-bit data from day 3 (`highbit/`). Display range (min/max) is part of the LUT; never let it silently clip data used for measurement.
- **Fluorescence** channels are grey images (one per filter set), not RGB: displaying them is a choice. **Pseudocolour / LUTs**: map intensity to a colour ramp per channel, merge additively. Red/green merges are invisible to ~8 % of men: use magenta/green or cyan/magenta/yellow; always show channels separately too.
- **Colour maps for scalar data**: lightness must be monotonic for sequential data (viridis, cividis), a diverging map for signed data (zero = neutral), a qualitative palette for labels. Jet/rainbow: non-monotonic $L^*$ creates false edges and hides real ones, and fails for colour-blind viewers. Picture: $L^*$ along each map; the same height field with jet vs viridis. These are the course's maps (`lib/colour.ts`).
- **Simulating colour blindness** as a linear map: in LMS, a dichromat's response is a projection onto a plane (Brettel/Viénot/Machado); one $3 \times 3$ matrix of rank 2 in linear RGB. Use it to check figures.
- **Decorrelation stretch** (recall `pca/` from day 1, now on colour pixels): RGB of natural images is strongly correlated (the cloud is a cigar along the grey axis). Rotate to the principal axes (eigenvectors of the $3 \times 3$ sample covariance $S$), equalise the variances, rotate back: faint colour differences become visible (remote sensing, faded stains, rock art). The first principal component as a data-driven greyscale.
- **Demo `colourmap`** (new, small): a grey image (fluorescence-like, a height field, a signed difference image), LUT menu (grey, viridis, cividis, magma, jet, a diverging map, a qualitative palette) with its $L^*$ profile plotted under the colour bar; display range sliders; *simulate deuteranopia / protanopia / tritanopia* toggle applied to the whole demo; a two- or three-channel fluorescence merge mode (red/green vs magenta/green). Presets: "jet invents edges", "viridis is monotone", "red/green merge for a deuteranope", "signed data needs a diverging map".
- **Demo**: reuse `colourcloud` with `data-mode="pca"`: the covariance ellipsoid and principal axes drawn in the cloud, a *decorrelation stretch* slider that morphs the cloud to the stretched one and updates the image.
- By hand: (a) $L^*$ at a few points along a given rainbow map vs viridis: which is monotone? (b) pick a diverging map's centre for data in $[-3, 7]$; (c) apply a given rank-2 dichromat matrix to red and green and check they collapse; (d) the principal axes of a given $3 \times 3$ colour covariance (diagonal or block-diagonal, so eigenvectors are read off).

Spectral tables compiled in as arrays at 5 or 10 nm: CIE 1931 $\bar{x}\bar{y}\bar{z}$, cone fundamentals, D65, Planck's law (computed), a synthetic camera; and the spectral locus for the xy diagram.
- Image representation: planar `Float32Array` channels in **linear light** internally; decode on load, encode only when drawing to the canvas (the canvas is sRGB). A helper pair `loadColour(img) → {r,g,b}` / `drawColour(canvas, {r,g,b}, encoded?)`, in `lib/imageops.ts` next to `loadGrey()`. Per-channel Gaussian blur and resampling reuse `imageops.ts` (owner `kernels/`).
- `lib/gl3d.ts` (owner: 3D-graphics elective; the shared WebGL renderer): needs a **point-cloud** primitive (per-vertex position and colour, point size, ~50k points), line segments for axes and gamut outlines, and vertex-position interpolation between two buffers for the morph animation (a uniform $t$ in the vertex shader). Context created only while visible, as that plan specifies. Fallback: static figure.
- `lib/linalg.ts` (owner: graph-cut day, which moves `jacobiEigen` there): $3 \times 3$ inverse, solve, cross product, small least squares (3×3 colour correction fit), and the shared symmetric eigendecomposition for PCA of colour pixels; null-space basis of a $3 \times n$ matrix (Gram–Schmidt / projection $I - A^\top(AA^\top)^{-1}A$) for the metamer button.
- `lib/histogram.ts` (owner thresholding): add a 2D histogram (two channels, bins, circular axis for hue) and its log-scale drawing; `lib/threshold.ts` (thresholding): Otsu on a derived channel; `lib/kmeans.ts` (owner GMM elective; first written here for `colourseg` if colour is built first, with weighted points); Dice from `lib/metrics.ts` (segmentation section, owner thresholding).
- `lib/plot.ts` (owner morphology): spectrum curves, colour bars with $L^*$ profiles, the xy diagram.
- Synthetic images (seeded, `rng(seed)` from `lib/random.ts`) in `lib/synth.ts` (owner thresholding): coloured objects with shading and noise plus ground truth; spectral chart scenes (smooth random reflectances). The Beer–Lambert H&E / H-DAB generator moves with `stainsep` to the unmixing elective.
- Performance: all per-pixel conversions on 256×256×3 are well under a few ms in TypeScript; the point cloud is a single buffer upload; ΔE2000 over all pixels is the heaviest step and is still fast at this size. No Wasm expected (measure against the ~30 ms rule).
- Tests (`demos/test/colour-test.ts`, run by `make test`): sRGB encode∘decode is the identity (and the 0.04045 knee is continuous); $M_{\text{sRGB}}\,(1,1,1)^\top$ = D65 white; the matrix built from primaries + white reproduces the published sRGB matrix; Lab of white = (100, 0, 0) and of greys has $a^* = b^* = 0$; HSV round trip away from grey; ΔE2000 against the published test pairs of Sharma, Wu & Dalal (2005); YCbCr round trip within rounding; the metamer button gives $A(\mathbf{s}_2 - \mathbf{s}_1) \approx 0$ and $\mathbf{s}_2 \ge 0$; grey world equalises channel means; 3×3 inverse times $M$ = identity; every sequential map has monotone $L^*$; the dichromat matrices have rank 2.
- Follow `demos/README.md`: fixed seeds, presets + reset, keyboard back to reveal.js (orbit rotation by pointer only), fits 1150×560 on a slide (image 256 px + 3D or histogram panel), labels ≥ 18 px, no hover-only information.
