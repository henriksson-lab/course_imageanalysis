# Plan: a day on the Hough transform (lines, circles, generalised shapes)

Internal working doc (plan/ is not rendered). Status: draft for discussion, 2026-11-12.

## Goal of the day

After the day a student can:

1. turn an image into a set of **edge points with a direction** (compact Canny: smooth, gradient, non-maximum suppression, hysteresis) and say what each step is for;
2. explain the **duality** behind the Hough transform: a point in the image is a curve in parameter space, and the curves of collinear points meet in one point; find the common $(\rho, \theta)$ of three points by hand;
3. explain why slope–intercept fails and the **normal parameterisation** $\rho = x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta$ works, and run **voting and peak finding** in an accumulator, choosing its resolution and threshold and predicting the effect of noise and clutter;
4. detect **circles** (3D parameter space $(a, b, r)$, 2D with known radius), use the **gradient direction** to cut the number of votes, and extend the idea to arbitrary shapes with the **generalised Hough transform** (R-table; rotation and scale as extra dimensions);
5. fit a model with outliers by **RANSAC**, compute the number of iterations needed, and compare voting with hypothesise-and-verify.

Why it fits this audience: everything is a change of variables, a sum of votes, and a
maximum; the duality is a line in one space becoming a point in another, which is
linear algebra they already know. The step from lines to circles to arbitrary shapes
is the same idea with more parameters, and makes the cost of each extra dimension
concrete. RANSAC needs only basic probability.

## Units (one folder each: lec, quiz, exercise with #explore)

Four core units (unit 1 about 1 h, the others about 1.5 h each including the exercise), plus one optional unit.

### 1. `canny/`: From pixels to edge points (Canny, compact)

- Concept layer. Picture: the gradient magnitude of `cell256.png` is a **thick, noisy ridge**, not a list of edge points. We want each edge once, one pixel wide, with its direction, and without the noise.
- Four steps, each with a picture and a one-line reason:
  1. **smooth** with a Gaussian of width $\sigma$ (recall `kernels/`): fewer noise edges, but corners round off and close edges merge;
  2. **gradient** (Sobel): magnitude = edge strength, direction = the normal of the edge;
  3. **non-maximum suppression** (NMS): keep a pixel only if its magnitude is at least that of its two neighbours *along the gradient direction* (direction rounded to 0°, 45°, 90°, 135°). The ridge becomes a one-pixel line. Worked on a 5×5 patch with numbers;
  4. **hysteresis** (recall, thresholding day): strong edges seed, weak edges survive only if connected.
- Output for the rest of the day: a list of edge points $(x_i, y_i)$, each with a direction $\theta_i$ and a strength.
- Failure modes from the demo: $\sigma$ too small (noise edges), too large (corners and close edges merge, T-junctions break), thresholds too high (gaps in edges, which hurt the Hough transform less than other methods: unit 2).
- **The math**: Canny's three criteria (detection, localisation, single response) in words and the result that the optimal detector is close to a derivative of Gaussian; NMS with sub-pixel interpolation along the gradient (bilinear) instead of rounding to four directions; one line on why the derivative of a Gaussian is the Gaussian smoothing of the derivative (convolution commutes).
- Pointer: image libraries call the pipeline "Canny".
- **Demo `canny`** (new): 128×128 image (synthetic: a rectangle, a disc, two close parallel edges and a T-junction with seeded noise; real: `cell256.png` downsampled). Stage selector *smoothed / magnitude / direction (colour wheel) / after NMS / after hysteresis*; sliders $\sigma$, noise, $t_\text{low}$, $t_\text{high}$. Click a pixel: inset shows the 3×3 neighbourhood, the gradient arrow, the two neighbours compared by NMS, and kept/removed. Presets: "σ too small: noise edges", "σ too large: close edges merge", "NMS thins the ridge", "hysteresis closes the gap", "micrograph".
- By hand: (a) on a given 5×5 magnitude + direction patch, apply NMS; (b) Sobel at two pixels of a 4×4 step image, giving magnitude and direction in degrees. *(math)*: sub-pixel NMS by bilinear interpolation at one pixel; show that $\partial_x(G_\sigma * I) = (\partial_x G_\sigma) * I$.

### 2. `hough-lines/`: Duality, the accumulator and line detection

- Concept layer, picture first: through one point pass infinitely many lines; each is described by two numbers, so the point "is" a curve in the space of line parameters. Two points: two curves, they cross at the one line through both. Three collinear points: three curves through a common point. **Detecting a line = finding where many curves meet.** Demo `houghdual` before any formula.
- **Slope–intercept fails**: $y = mx + c$ gives the nice picture (a point becomes a straight line in $(m, c)$) but vertical lines have $m = \infty$ and $m$ is unbounded, so it cannot be a finite grid. Shown in the demo by dragging three points towards a vertical line.
- **Normal parameterisation**: a line is the set of points whose projection onto the unit normal $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ equals $\rho$: $\rho = x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta$, $\theta \in [0, \pi)$, $|\rho| \le$ the image diagonal. Bounded. A point becomes a **sinusoid** in $(\theta, \rho)$. Small worked example: points $(2,0), (1,1), (0,2)$ at $\theta = 0°, 45°, 90°, 135°$; only $\theta = 45°$ gives the same $\rho = \sqrt2$ for all three.
- **The accumulator**: a grid of bins $(\theta_j, \rho_k)$, every edge point adds one vote to each bin its sinusoid passes through; lines are **peaks**. Gaps in a line do not matter (votes just add), which is the strength of the method.
- **Peak finding**: threshold on the vote count (≈ minimum line length in pixels) plus **non-maximum suppression in parameter space** (keep local maxima in a window; the same idea as unit 1, and as regional maxima on the morphology day). Without NMS one line gives a cluster of detections.
- **Resolution vs precision**: bins too coarse merge nearby lines and give a poor angle; too fine, votes of one noisy line spread over many bins and the peak vanishes. Rule of thumb in pictures: $\Delta\rho \approx 1$ px, $\Delta\theta$ such that the far end of the line moves about one pixel. Noise in the edge positions widens the peak; clutter (texture, other objects) raises the floor of the accumulator.
- **Use the gradient direction**: the gradient *is* the normal of the edge, so $\theta_i$ is known from unit 1. Vote only near $\theta_i$ (one bin, or a small window for its uncertainty). Votes per point: from $N_\theta$ (e.g. 180) to about 1–5; clutter votes no longer pile up by chance.
- **Lines vs segments**: a peak is an infinite line. Endpoints: walk along the line, collect the edge points within a pixel of it, split at gaps longer than $g$, keep pieces longer than $L_\text{min}$.
- Applications on one slide each, with the demo preset: lane / road edges and building edges in a photograph; **deskewing** a scanned page or a tilted micrograph (dominant $\theta$ = the peak of the column sums of the accumulator); **filaments** (microtubules, stress fibres: straight segments in clutter).
- **The math**: the Hough transform as a sum $H(\theta, \rho) = \sum_i [\,|x_i\cos\theta + y_i\sin\theta - \rho| < \Delta\rho/2\,]$; the sinusoid $\rho = A\cos(\theta - \varphi)$ with $A = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, $\varphi = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x)$ (amplitude and phase from the point's polar coordinates); two points' sinusoids intersect at the normal of the line through them (solve the $2\times2$ system); cost $O(N_\text{edge} N_\theta)$ vs $O(N_\text{edge})$ with the gradient; angle precision of a segment of length $L$ with endpoint noise $\sigma$ is about $\sigma\sqrt{2}/L$ radians, which sets a useful $\Delta\theta$; projective duality: in homogeneous coordinates a line $\mathbf{l}$ and a point $\mathbf{x}$ satisfy $\mathbf{l}^\top\mathbf{x} = 0$, symmetric in both (pointer to the graphics 3D day).
- **Demo `houghdual`** (new): left, an image plane with up to 8 draggable points (click to add, right-click or a button to remove); right, the $(\theta, \rho)$ plane with one coloured sinusoid per point. Hovering is not needed: click a point in parameter space to draw that line in the image; the intersection of all curves is marked when the points are collinear. Toggle *parameterisation: slope–intercept / normal* (in slope–intercept the curves are straight lines and run off the panel as the points approach a vertical line). Toggle *accumulator* overlays the binned votes with a $\Delta\theta, \Delta\rho$ slider. Presets: "three collinear points", "two points: one line", "towards vertical: slope–intercept breaks", "four points, two lines: two crossings", "noisy collinear: the curves almost meet", "coarse vs fine bins".
- **Demo `houghlines`** (new, main demo of the unit): three panels: image with edge points (from `canny`, with its $\sigma$ and thresholds collapsed into one *edges* setting), accumulator (heat map, detected peaks circled), detected lines or segments drawn on the image. Readouts: number of votes cast, time, detections. Images: synthetic (a few lines, a grid, a road with lane markings, a rotated striped "page", seeded straight filaments in clutter) and `cell256.png`. Presets: "clean lines", "no NMS: clusters", "bins too fine: peak dissolves", "bins too coarse: lines merge", "clutter raises the floor", "gradient voting: 100× fewer votes", "segments with gaps", "deskew the page", "filaments".

(c) two parallel lines 3 px apart with $\Delta\rho = 4$: what is detected? *(math)*: (d) derive the intersection of the sinusoids of $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$; (e) amplitude–phase form of the sinusoid; (f) votes and memory for a 512×512 image with 10 000 edge points, $\Delta\theta = 0.5°$, with and without gradient voting.

### 3. `hough-shapes/`: Circles, ellipses and the generalised Hough transform

- Concept layer. **Circles**: $(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2$, three parameters. With a **known radius**, every edge point votes for all centres at distance $r$ from it: a circle of votes in the $(a, b)$ plane, and the circles of votes of all points on a true circle meet at its centre. A 2D accumulator that looks like the image. Picture and demo first.
- **Unknown radius**: one $(a, b)$ slice per radius, a 3D accumulator (a cone of votes per point). Memory and time grow with every parameter: e.g. 256×256×40 radii ≈ 2.6 M bins.
- **Gradient direction** again: the centre lies along the gradient, at distance $r$ in front of or behind the point (dark-on-bright vs bright-on-dark). Two votes per radius instead of $\approx 2\pi r$; with known polarity, one. Collapsed variant: vote along the gradient ray for all radii into a 2D centre map, then estimate $r$ per centre from a radius histogram.
- Applications (each a preset): nuclei and round cells; bacterial / yeast **colonies** on a plate; **wells** of a multi-well plate (known radius, plus a regular grid); vesicles; **powder diffraction rings** (concentric circles with a known centre: the accumulator reduces to a 1D histogram of radius).
- Failure modes: touching or overlapping circles (shared votes, false centres between them), non-circular cells (a smeared peak), a wrong radius range, strong straight edges nearby.
- **Ellipses** briefly: five parameters; the full accumulator is impractical. Two ideas named: use pairs of points with parallel gradients (their midpoints lie on a line through the centre) to find the centre first, then the rest; or hypothesise-and-verify (unit 4).
- **Generalised Hough transform** (Ballard 1981): an arbitrary shape has no formula, so we store it as a table. Pick a reference point inside the template. For every edge point of the template, record the vector to the reference point, indexed by the edge direction: the **R-table**. In a new image, every edge point looks up the vectors listed for its direction and votes for the reference points they give. A peak = the shape is there. Worked on a small polygon with 4 directions.
- The circle with gradient voting *is* a GHT whose R-table holds one vector per direction (length $r$, along the gradient): the general method contains the special case.
- **Rotation and scale** as extra dimensions: rotate/scale the R-table vectors and the indexing direction, one accumulator slice per $(\phi, s)$; 2D becomes 4D. Cost and the remedy of coarse-to-fine search.
- Comparison slide: GHT (global, finds all copies, tolerates occlusion, fixed shape) vs active shape models (local, needs initial pose, shape varies; snakes day) vs moment invariants (moments day) vs polar DP (free-form star-shaped boundary from a centre; DP day). Where this leads: **Hough forests** learn which local patches vote for which object centre (one line).
- **The math**: the circle accumulator as a sum over edge points of a ring kernel, i.e. a **convolution** of the edge map with a ring of radius $r$ (link to `kernels/`; and with gradient voting the convolution of $\nabla I$ with a vector field); bins per dimension and the exponential growth $N^d$ of the accumulator; GHT with similarity transform $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{x} - sR(\phi)\,\mathbf{v}$, $\phi$-shift of the table index; GHT as **matched filtering**: with all directions merged it is correlation of the edge map with the template's edge map; ellipse: the conic $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$ has 5 degrees of freedom.
- **Demo `houghcircle`** (new): left, image with edge points and detections; middle, the accumulator slice at the radius on the slider (heat map, peaks marked); right, the maximum over all radii (or, toggle, a 3D view of the $(a, b, r)$ accumulator as stacked slices via `lib/gl3d.ts`). Sliders: radius (slice shown), radius range $[r_\text{min}, r_\text{max}]$, vote threshold, NMS window, noise; toggles *gradient voting*, *polarity: dark-on-bright / bright-on-dark / either*. Click on an edge point to see its votes (a ring, or two dots per radius with gradient voting). Images: seeded synthetic nuclei (discs of several radii, touching pairs, one ellipse, partial occlusion; from the nuclei generator in `lib/imageops.ts`), a synthetic multi-well plate, a synthetic powder-diffraction ring pattern, and `cell256.png`. Readouts: votes cast, time. Presets: "one radius: a sharp peak", "wrong radius: a ring, no peak", "scan the radius", "gradient voting", "touching cells: a false centre", "ellipse: smeared peak", "plate wells", "diffraction rings", "micrograph nuclei". TypeScript first; measure the 3D case without gradient voting at 256×256 (roughly 5 000 edge points × 40 radii × $2\pi r$ ≈ 10⁷ votes): only if it exceeds ~30 ms after optimisation does the inner loop move to Wasm.
- **Demo `ght`** (new): left, the template (presets: a key, a leaf, a letter "R", a gear; editable as a polygon with draggable vertices) with its reference point (draggable) and the R-table drawn as arrows per direction bin, plus the table itself (direction bin → list of vectors); right, a scene with copies of the template (translated, optionally rotated and scaled), partial occlusion, clutter shapes and noise, the accumulator and detections. Presets: "translation only", "occluded copy still found", "rotation without rotation search: missed", "rotation search: found, 36× the work", "similar clutter shape: false positive", "circle template = circle Hough".
- By hand: (a) radius 5, edge point $(3, 4)$ with unit gradient $(0.6, 0.8)$: the two candidate centres; then three edge points of a circle with given gradients: vote and find the centre; (b) build the R-table of a small square (4 directions) with the reference at its centre, and vote with the edges of a translated square; (c) predict: how many accumulator bins and votes for 300×300, radii 5–40, with and without gradient voting? *(math)*: (d) show that the known-radius circle Hough is the convolution of the edge map with a ring; (e) how the R-table changes under rotation by $\phi$ and scaling by $s$; (f) degrees of freedom of a conic and why the ellipse accumulator is 5D.

### 4. `ransac/`: Hypothesise and verify: RANSAC and randomised Hough

- Concept layer. Picture: a least-squares line through points with 30 % outliers is pulled away; the Hough transform solves it by voting, but needs a grid in parameter space. Other idea: **guess and check**.
- **RANSAC** (Fischler & Bolles 1981): repeat — pick a **minimal sample** (2 points for a line, 3 for a circle); fit the model exactly; count the points within a distance $\varepsilon$ (the **inliers**); keep the model with most inliers. At the end, refit by least squares on its inliers only.
- Parameters and failure modes: $\varepsilon$ too small (true points rejected), too large (outliers accepted, two nearby lines merged); not enough iterations; several models in the data (sequential RANSAC: remove inliers, run again) — Hough finds all at once.
- **Randomised Hough** (Xu, Oja & Kultanen 1990) as the bridge: pick a minimal sample, fit, and cast *one* vote for that parameter value (sparse accumulator). **Probabilistic Hough** (Kiryati et al. 1991; progressive variant Matas et al. 2000): vote with a random subset of edge points and stop once a peak is clear. Pointer: image libraries' "probabilistic Hough" returns segments.
- **Comparison** (table): voting (Hough) vs hypothesise-and-verify (RANSAC): number of parameters (Hough suffers $N^d$, RANSAC does not), many instances (Hough natural, RANSAC sequential), outlier fraction (Hough copes with very high fractions, RANSAC's iterations explode), precision (Hough limited by bins, RANSAC + refit is continuous), determinism (Hough deterministic, RANSAC random: fixed seeds in the demo). Where RANSAC is standard: fitting geometric transforms between images from matched points (registration, panoramas): one-line pointer.
- **The math**: with inlier fraction $w$ and sample size $s$, one sample is all-inlier with probability $w^s$; after $N$ samples at least one is with probability $1 - (1 - w^s)^N$; so $N = \log(1 - p)/\log(1 - w^s)$. Worked: $w = 0.5$, $s = 2$, $p = 0.99$ gives $N = 17$; $s = 3$ gives 35; $w = 0.2$, $s = 2$ gives 113. Least-squares refit: total least squares for a line (the normal is the eigenvector of the scatter matrix with the smaller eigenvalue: unit 2's $(\theta, \rho)$ again), algebraic circle fit (linear in $(a, b, c)$ with $c = r^2 - a^2 - b^2$); MSAC / robust loss in one line.
- **Demo `ransac`** (new): a point set (draggable; add / remove points) with inliers on a line or a circle and seeded outliers; model *line / circle*. Buttons *one sample* (the two or three chosen points highlighted, the model drawn with its $\varepsilon$ band, inliers counted), *run N*, *reset*. Sliders $\varepsilon$, outlier fraction, iterations, seed. Panels: best-so-far model and inlier count vs iteration; least squares on all points vs RANSAC + refit; the curve $1 - (1 - w^s)^N$ against $N$ with the current run marked; side toggle *same points in the Hough accumulator* (reusing `lib/hough.ts`). Presets: "least squares fails", "RANSAC: 17 iterations suffice", "80 % outliers: Hough still works, RANSAC needs hundreds", "ε too large: merges two lines", "two lines: sequential RANSAC", "circle".
- By hand: (a) 8 given points, two given samples: fit the line through each, count inliers with $\varepsilon = 0.5$, choose; (b) compute $N$ for the three worked cases above and for a circle with $w = 0.3$; (c) predict: which is better for a line in 90 % clutter, and which for a 6-parameter model with 40 % outliers? *(math)*: (d) derive the formula for $N$; (e) show that the total-least-squares line normal is an eigenvector of the scatter matrix; (f) write the algebraic circle fit as a linear least-squares problem.
